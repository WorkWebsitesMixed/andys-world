Drawn for 5.4 mm board with slots at 5.50 mm. The geometry is true nominal — no kerf compensation is applied in the file, because the shop applies its own. A 5.50 slot should measure 5.50 in the board.

Filenames have never changed across revisions, so make the technician delete any copy they already hold. Every version of this sheet looks identical at a glance and every slot on it differs by fractions of a millimetre — which is the whole difference between a kit that assembles and one that does not.

02Before the sheet goes on the bed

  1. Measure the board. Callipers, three places, take the largest reading. Every slot, tab and nut pocket in the file is derived from that one number, and “5 mm” MDF is routinely 5.3 to 5.6.

  2. Cut the comb and settle the slot width. Seven slots, labelled in hundredths of a millimetre, spanning 0.05 mm under the board thickness to 0.25 over it. Push an offcut of the real sheet into each and find the narrowest one it enters without force. That label is your drawn width — no arithmetic and no kerf to reason about, because the comb went through the same machine and the same CAM the sheet will:

    python3 facade.py --thickness 5.4 --slot 5.50
python3 check.py  --thickness 5.4 --slot 5.50

    Millimetres, not the label’s hundredths. The second line matters: run the checker with the same flags or you validate a different sheet from the one you cut.

  3. Set the cut order to inner-first. Every small part nests inside its own façade’s door aperture. If the aperture is cut before the parts inside it, the slug drops and those parts get cut on a fallen piece. In LightBurn: Optimisation Settings → Cut inner shapes first.

03The façade

The façade is a jig, not a model. It hands every group the same doorway and the same hinge axis, so any student’s door fits any building and swaps in a minute on two M3 bolts. Everything else is theirs.

All 24 window panes cut through, and each upper window has four M3 holes at its corners. Nothing is glazed now; whatever LED scheme turns up in a later term bolts to those holes without a single new part.

04The deck and the T-slot field

There is deliberately no suggested motor position engraved anywhere. With 25 students owning their own geometry, an outline on the board would anchor all of them to one answer. What the deck gives instead is a field: four slots, 300 mm of continuous travel each, four depths. A crank student sets the motor back; a cam student brings it up close behind the door. Neither is decided for them.

The scored quarter-circle is the one thing that is prescriptive, and it is a physical fact rather than a suggestion: it is the volume the door sweeps through. A linkage designed into it will not work.

05Head plate and gussets

A vertical hinge pin cannot pass through a vertical panel, which is why the head bearing plate exists. It tabs through the façade above the doorway and does two jobs at once: it is the doorway soffit, and it carries the upper pivot directly above the deck’s lower one. It is also why the door swings inward — which is what firefighters do to a house door anyway, and it keeps the leaf out of the mechanism space.

The gussets hold the façade square. They bolt through captive nuts: the nut drops into a pocket in the gusset’s face and the bolt enters from the façade, so nothing is ever threaded into MDF edge grain, which strips. Drop each nut into its pocket before you start the bolt. The two heads land on the scored corner boards, where they read as pipe brackets.

07The motor cradle

The cradle is three parts — one base and two saddles — and it exists to solve a problem the store cupboard creates: you do not know exactly which motor will turn up. Rather than a hole sized to one gearbox, the saddles are open Us. The motor drops in from above and a zip tie holds it down. A different motor next year needs no new part.

Building it

  1. Saddles into the base. Two tabs each, into the four slots, U-notches facing up. They only go in one way round. Slip fit, no glue — students will want to take this apart when their first geometry does not work.

  2. Motor into the U’s. It lies across both saddles with the gearbox flat, output shaft overhanging one end. The U is 23 mm wide and 20 deep; a TT gearmotor fits with almost nothing to spare. If your motor is fatter, that pair of numbers lives in cradle_saddle() and is the only thing to change.

  3. Zip tie over the top. One tie per saddle, up through the slot on one side, over the motor body, down through the other. Pull it tight enough that the motor cannot climb out under load — a stalling gearmotor kicks harder than students expect.

  4. Cradle onto the deck. Two M4 bolts up through a deck T-slot, through the base’s long slots, nuts on top. Leave them finger-tight until the mechanism works, so the whole cradle can still slide along the slot and shuffle across it. That two-axis adjustment is the point of the part.

The consequence worth teaching

The cradle holds the shaft horizontal. The door hinges on a vertical axis. Those two facts do not combine for free, and noticing that is most of the mechanism lesson:

ApproachHow it lands
CamWorks straight out of the cradle. A disc on the horizontal shaft turns in a vertical plane and pushes a follower on the door’s inner face. Fewest parts, most forgiving of sloppy tolerances. This is the path most groups will find.
Crank in the cradleThe crank pin sweeps a vertical circle while the door needs to move horizontally. It can be made to work with a link loose enough to swivel at both ends, but it fights the geometry.
Crank on a vertical axisThe clean four-bar answer, and it needs a mount that stands the motor with its shaft pointing up — which the cradle does not do. Students who want it design and cut it. There is spare stock in the gable off-cuts for exactly this.
Why not just supply a vertical mount too

Because handing out both would quietly tell 25 students that there are exactly two right answers. The constraint as it stands is honest and visible: here is a horizontal shaft, there is a vertical hinge, close the gap. Point groups at the off-cut pile rather than at a part.

Before power goes anywhere near it

  • Door swings freely by hand, through the full sweep, with the motor disconnected. A door that binds will stall the motor and the motor will win.
  • Nothing sits inside the scored arc on the deck.
  • The mechanism cannot drive the door into a hard stop. A gearmotor at stall draws several times its running current and will cook whatever is switching it.
  • Gearbox backlash is normal. Expect a few degrees of free play at the shaft; design the linkage so that slack does not matter.
  • Cradle bolts finger-tight during testing, fully tight only once the geometry is settled.
Circuit, not façade

The BC547 taught in Weeks 8–9 is a 100 mA part. A TT gearmotor stalls near 700 mA, and the first door that jams will destroy it. A BD139 or TIP120 drops into the identical position and nothing you teach changes — same base-resistor reasoning, same thermistor divider, same explanation of the transistor making the decision.

08Assembly order

  1. Façade into the deck. Four tabs, friction fit. Square it by eye.

  2. Gussets. Tab down into the deck, then M4 × 25 from the front into the captive nuts. Nut into the pocket first.

  3. Head bearing plate. Tabs through the façade above the doorway, then two M3 × 25 from the front into its captive nuts. Until these are in, it hangs loose.

  4. Doublers. One over each pivot — on top of the head plate, underneath the deck.

  5. The student’s door hangs on two M3 × 20 driven from outside: down through the head plate, up through the deck. No rod to thread, nothing to spring into place.

  6. Nameplate over its two pegs, and the mechanism goes in last, through the open back.

09How the sheet is packed

Three kits fit because nothing is wasted: every small part lives inside its own façade’s door aperture, which is 140 × 315 of scrap that would otherwise be binned. The right-hand strip takes the gussets nested in pairs, the head plates turned 90°, and three blank nameplate tiles for when you know your bench groups.

The gable off-cuts are left whole rather than filled with parts. That is the reserve stock — door leaves, cams, cranks, and the vertical motor mount a group will eventually want to design.

10Section tiles

The address is engraved permanently — same house for everyone, it is part of the fiction. The section is a separate tile on two keyholes. 10A, 10B, 10C are cut now; when you know your bench groups, a strip of new tiles comes off any off-cut in about ninety seconds. No façade is ever re-cut because a group name changed.
