Electronics starts from zero this week. You do not need any prior knowledge: every symbol and idea is introduced before you use it. By the end of the session you will have rescued an LED: you will have read resistors by their colours, calculated the resistor that protects the LED, drawn the circuit with the correct symbols, built it, and had it tested at a clinic desk. The Firefighter Barbie circuit uses these same symbols and the same calculation later in the project.

<Callout type="exam" title="IPO and specific language" id="why">
  Paper 1 Section A regularly asks you to label a circuit using the input-process-output model and to name the function of each component, and it asks for the value of a current-limiting resistor with every step shown. Generic answers, such as "it makes the circuit work", earn zero. Name the component, state what it does, and use the correct technical term: thermistor, transistor, motor, never "sensor thing" or "switch part".
</Callout>

<Section title="Circuit symbols and conventions" icon="🔧" id="symbols" step="2–3">

### The eight symbols you must know

Copy these onto your reference card. They are the BS/IEC symbols that the exam papers use.

<CircuitSymbolsDiagram />

The same eight in words:

| Component | Symbol description | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cell (single) | Long thin line (positive) + short thick line (negative) | Single power source |
| Battery | Multiple cell symbols in series | Multiple cells providing higher voltage |
| Switch (open) | Line with a gap and a lever | Breaks the circuit when open |
| Resistor | Plain rectangle (not a zigzag) | Limits current flow |
| LED | Diode triangle with a bar, two arrows pointing away | Emits light when current flows in forward direction |
| Motor | Circle with M inside | Converts electrical energy to rotational mechanical energy |
| Thermistor | Resistor rectangle with a diagonal line through it | Resistance changes with temperature |
| NPN transistor | Circle with base, collector, emitter lines | Amplifies or switches current, controlled by base current |

Draw each symbol on your reference card. Size them consistently. A symbol that is too small or distorted will not be credited in an exam.

<Notebook title="Conventional current and conductors">

Current flows from positive to negative terminal in a circuit diagram (conventional direction). The wire (conductor) allows current to flow; the plastic insulation around it prevents current leaking to unwanted paths.

</Notebook>

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Labelling a circuit with IPO" id="worked-example" step="6">
**Circuit:** Cell → switch → resistor → LED → back to cell.

<CircuitDiagram />

**IPO labelling:**
- **Input:** Switch (the human trigger: on/off control)
- **Process:** Resistor (limits current to protect the LED)
- **Output:** LED (produces light, the visible result)

**Applied to Firefighter Barbie:**
- **Input:** Thermistor (detects heat, changes resistance as temperature rises)
- **Process:** Transistor circuit (makes the switching decision, turns on when base voltage exceeds threshold)
- **Output:** DC motor (converts electrical energy to rotation, drives the crank or cam mechanism)

The IPO model helps you see that each component has a role in the system, not just a name.
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="Reading a resistor: the colour code" icon="🎨" id="colour-code" step="4">

### Four bands, four jobs

A resistor is too small to print a number on, so its value is painted as coloured bands. Hold it with the **gold or silver band on the right** and read from the left.

<Notebook title="Reading a four-band resistor">

- **Band 1 and band 2:** the first two digits (black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9).
- **Band 3:** the multiplier (brown ×10, red ×100, orange ×1 000, yellow ×10 000).
- **Band 4:** the tolerance (gold ±5%, silver ±10%).
- **Example:** yellow, violet, red, gold = 47 × 100 = 4 700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ, ±5%.

</Notebook>

The full colour table and the standard values are on your colour card. You do not need to memorise them today: you will use the card until you do not need it.

### Units: the mistake that costs marks every year

1 kΩ = 1 000 Ω and 1 MΩ = 1 000 000 Ω. Convert to ohms **before** you substitute into a formula. Paper 4 asks for conversions such as 5 kΩ, 0.27 kΩ and 3 MΩ in ohms.

### You cannot buy every value

Resistors come in standard steps called the E12 series: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82, then the same numbers ×10, ×100, ×1 000. A calculated 360 Ω does not exist in the shop, so you take the **next value up**: 390 Ω.

</Section>

<Section title="Ohm's law and the LED resistor" icon="🔧" id="ohms-law" step="5–6">

<Notebook title="Ohm's law">

Three forms, one formula:

- $V = I \times R$ (find voltage if you know current and resistance)
- $I = \dfrac{V}{R}$ (find current if you know voltage and resistance)
- $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ (find resistance if you know voltage and current)

</Notebook>

### Calculating a current-limiting resistor for an LED

An LED has two critical values:
- **Forward voltage (VLED):** the voltage it drops when operating (about 2 V for red, yellow and green, about 3 V for blue and white).
- **Forward current (ILED):** the current it needs to light safely (typically 10 to 20 mA; 15 mA is a good design value).

The resistor must drop the remaining voltage above the LED's forward voltage:

<Notebook title="Current-limiting resistor">

$$R = \dfrac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}$$

After calculating R, always choose the **nearest standard resistor value above** the calculated value, then check the real current with $I = \dfrac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{R}$.

</Notebook>

Today's supply is the **7.4 V battery pack**: two 18650 cells in series. A fully charged pack reads about 8.4 V, so always check that the current is still safe at the top of the range.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Calculating R for an LED" id="worked-r" step="5–6">
**Given:** Supply voltage = 7.4 V. LED forward voltage = 2 V. LED current = 8 mA.

**Step 1: convert mA to A** before substituting: $8\ \text{mA} = 0.008\ \text{A}$.

**Step 2: formula, substitution, result:**

<Formula
  name="Current-limiting resistor"
  equation="R = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "R", meaning: "the resistance of the current-limiting resistor fitted in series with the LED", unit: "Ω" },
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{supply}}", meaning: "the battery voltage feeding the circuit", unit: "V" },
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{LED}}", meaning: "the forward voltage the LED itself drops: about 2 V for red, yellow and green, about 3 V for blue and white. Subtracted because the LED has already used that much of the supply", unit: "V" },
    { symbol: "I_{\\text{LED}}", meaning: "the current you want through the LED. Convert to amps first: 8 mA is 0.008 A. This is the single most common place to lose the mark", unit: "A" },
  ]}
  substitution="R = \frac{7.4\ \text{V} - 2\ \text{V}}{0.008\ \text{A}} = \frac{5.4\ \text{V}}{0.008\ \text{A}}"
  result="R = 675\ \Omega"
/>

**Step 3: choose the nearest standard value above 675 Ω.** From the E12 series that is 680 Ω.

**Step 4: check the current.** $I = \dfrac{5.4\ \text{V}}{680\ \Omega} = 7.9\ \text{mA}$, just below the 8 mA wanted. On a fully charged 8.4 V pack it is 9.4 mA, still inside the 20% the clinic allows.

**Answer:** use a 680 Ω resistor. Colours: blue, grey, brown, gold.
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="The LED rescue" icon="🛟" id="rescue" step="6">

### How the rescue works

Every pair has a different client. The sheet tells you the product, the LED colour, its forward voltage and the current the client wants. The supply is always the 7.4 V pack.

1. **Draw** the circuit with the correct symbols: cell or battery, switch, resistor, LED. Label input, process, output and the direction of conventional current.
2. **Calculate** with every step written: formula, substitution with units, result, standard value, predicted current.
3. **Choose** the resistor from the bin by reading its colours. The bin has decoys on purpose, and two of them would overdrive your LED.
4. **Build** from your own diagram, with the pack unplugged. Connect the pack last.
5. **Clinic:** take the board to a clinic desk. Measure the resistance out of the circuit, the voltage across it and the current through the loop, and compare $V \div I$ with the number you read from the colours.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Every LED in everything you own has a resistor like this one.** The status light on a charger, the standby light on a television and the glow on a phone case are all an LED with a resistor chosen by exactly this calculation. Choosing the wrong one is not a theory mistake: too small and the light dies in a flash, as you saw at the start; too large and it is so dim nobody notices it is on.
</Callout>

<Callout type="exam" title="Battery pack rules">
Two 18650 cells can deliver far more current than a 9 V battery if the rails touch. Build with the pack unplugged, connect it last and disconnect it first. Never lay a bare lead across both rails, and tell your teacher at once if anything gets warm.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Support title="Scaffolds for circuit drawing and building" id="scaffolds" step="3–6">
**Symbol reference card:** keep the symbol card open while you draw. In the exam you will not have it, which is why the pictionary at the end is done with everything closed.

**Pre-drawn circuit diagram with blanks:** if drawing the whole circuit is too open-ended, ask for the version where the circuit is printed and you add the IPO labels and the missing symbol.

**Sentence frame for rewriting:** "[Component] converts [input] into [output] by [mechanism/process], which means [consequence for the circuit]."

**Stuck on the calculation:** work the four steps in order and do not skip the first. Write what you know, convert mA to A, substitute, calculate, then go up to the next standard value. The three most common errors are forgetting to subtract the LED voltage, using mA instead of A, and rounding down.

**Before you touch a breadboard:** your board has 400 holes. The four long rails (red + and blue −) run the full length, and each strip of five holes on either side of the channel is joined inside. The two halves are not joined to each other. Side 1 of your colour card draws all of this with an example circuit. Most first-lesson failures are a lead one strip out, not a faulty component.

**LED will not light:** work through this list in order. Polarity (the long leg is the anode). The switch actually breaking the circuit rather than sitting across a rail. Resistor present. Battery clip seated. Every lead in the correct strip. Nine times in ten it is polarity or a lead one strip out.

**At the clinic desk:** voltage is measured *across* the part with the meter in parallel; current is measured *through* it with the meter in series, which means opening the circuit to put the meter in the gap. Never put the meter on its current range straight across the battery.
</Support>

**Quick-fire six (self-marked):** Answer these without notes, then check against the answers in the support box below.
1. Name the component whose symbol is a plain rectangle, and state its job.
2. On the symbol for a cell, which line is positive?
3. Write 4.7 kΩ and 0.27 kΩ in ohms.

<Support title="Quick-fire six: check your answers">
1. A resistor; it limits the current.
2. The long thin line.
3. 4 700 Ω and 270 Ω.
4. 1 000 Ω = 1 kΩ, with a tolerance of ±5%.
5. A motor.
6. R = (7.4 − 2) / 0.020 = 5.4 / 0.020 = 270 Ω. 270 Ω is a standard value, so use 270 Ω.

Rewrite any answer you got wrong using the specific term, not a general word like "moves" or "changes". For every one you missed, write the correct answer in full.
</Support>
