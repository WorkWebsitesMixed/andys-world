The building is already built. The firefighter is already on the way. Your job is to design the door — and the simple system that opens it by itself, the moment things get dangerous. This week: meet the brief, start your project folder, and learn the sketching trick you'll use all term.

<Callout type="exam" title="Design brief vs specification" id="why">
  Examiners check that you know the difference. A **brief** is one or two sentences: what you're designing, who it's for, and why. A **specification** (coming in Week 3) is a numbered list of exact requirements the design has to meet. Get the brief right first — the detail comes later.
</Callout>

Each part of the session says whether it is **individual, in pairs, or in a group of 4**, both here and in the section it belongs to. Everything in a bordered table or a `Formula` box goes into your notebook — those are the definitions and equations you are examined on.
</Callout>

<Section title="Project launch: Firefighter Barbie" icon="🔧" id="launch">

It's the middle of the night at 13 Ember Lane. Smoke is filling the hallway. Someone inside can't see, can barely breathe, and can't get the door open — their hands are shaking too hard to find the handle.

Outside, a fire truck is already pulling up. The firefighter running toward that door is wearing thick gloves and a breathing mask, and can barely see through the smoke either. There's no time to hunt for a handle — and gloved hands couldn't turn one properly anyway.

Neither person can open that door themselves. So it has to open itself.

That's the whole idea behind this project: a heat sensor feels the fire, and the moment it gets dangerously hot, the door opens on its own — no handle, no hesitation, nobody touching anything.

Your job this term is to design that door, and the mechanism and simple circuit that make it open by itself.

### What's fixed for you, and what's your choice

| Fixed by the brief — not your choice | Open — you decide |
|---|---|
| The building façade (it's already built for you) | The exact shape and size of your mechanism |
| The door has to open by itself when it gets hot | What your door panel looks like |
| A motor drives a crank or a cam to move the door | The exact circuit values you use |
| A heat sensor (a thermistor) triggers the switch | Where you mount the motor |
| Materials — pick from the workshop's palette | Any decoration you add |

### The design process in this project

You'll move through this cycle across all ten weeks. Right now you're at **Identify** — understanding who needs this and why. By Week 3 you'll be at **Specify**. You start actually making things in Week 10.

<Callout type="tip" title="The sixty-second emergency · 12 min · groups of 4">
Sixty seconds to list everything that has to happen for a firefighter to get through the locked door of a burning building. No discussion time — just write. Then read the lists out.

Between you, the room will have named the whole problem — heat, time, the occupant inside, the fact that nobody is there to turn a handle — before anyone has explained anything to you.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Sort the deck · 12 min · groups of 4">
Twelve cards, each a statement about the door system. Race to sort them into **brief** and **specification**. The first group finished reads their sort aloud and the room challenges any card it disagrees with.

Purpose versus measurable requirement is examined every year, and arguing about a card is how it sticks.
</Callout>

<Section title="Your user and context, in detail" icon="🧑‍🚒" id="user-context">

### 13 Ember Lane

Every student in Grade 10 designs for the same building: **13 Ember Lane**. It isn't a real address — but treat it like one. A specific place is easier to picture than a vague one. The class façade has three house-fronts on it (10A / 10B / 10C), one per bench group, so your door and mechanism belong to one real section of one real building.

### Two users, not one

Most design projects have a single user in mind. This one has **two — and their needs don't always line up**. Your design has to work for both at once.

| User | Their situation | What they need from the door |
|---|---|---|
| **The occupant**, inside | Trapped by smoke and heat. Could be a child, an older person, someone hurt, or just too scared to work a handle | A door that **opens itself** — no handle to operate, the moment things get dangerous |
| **The firefighter**, arriving | Wearing thick gloves and a breathing mask, moving through smoke, hands already full of gear | A door that's **already opening** by the time they arrive — nothing to find, nothing to operate |

Both point to the same answer: the door has to open **by itself**, **triggered by heat**, with **no one touching it**. That's why the brief already decides how it opens, instead of leaving it up to you — it's the one solution that genuinely works for both people.

### The façade you're building into

The façade — walls, roof, door opening, hinge — is built for the whole class: laser-cut wood, one sheet per three bench groups. It fixes exactly **two** things: the size of the door opening, and where the hinge goes. That's so any student's door can be fitted into it in Week 10, whatever they've designed. Everything else inside that opening is yours: the door itself, the mechanism, where you mount the motor, and how it looks.

### How long you have

This project runs for **Term 1 only** — ten weeks from brief to a working, tested system, finishing with your Week 10 evaluation. It's not the big year-long project — that comes in Grade 11, where it's worth half your final grade and you get to pick your own user. This term is where you practise the whole process — identify, specify, design, plan, make, test — on a scale you can finish in one term, before it counts for real.

<WorkedExample title="Writing a user/context statement">
**Context:** The Firefighter Barbie scenario.

**Weak version (too vague):**
"A firefighter needs to open a door in a building."

**Strong version (gives the brief real weight):**
"The building occupant may be trapped and unable to open the door manually. The firefighter arriving at the scene needs the door to open automatically when heat exceeds a threshold — without any manual contact — so that entry can happen immediately even if visibility is poor or the door handle is too hot to touch."

Notice: the strong version names who it's for (occupant and firefighter), the situation (fire, poor visibility, a hot handle), and what the design must do (open by itself, no touching, triggered by heat).
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="Exam practice — sketching fundamentals 1" icon="✏️" id="sketching">

Confident freehand sketching is the fastest way to show a design idea on Paper 1. Three basics this session:

1. **Line confidence** — draw straight lines in one smooth stroke, not back-and-forth scratching. Try "ghosting": hover the pencil along the path first, before it touches the paper.
2. **Ellipses** — in a sketch, circles look like ellipses. Draw them in one oval sweep, not two separate arcs. Getting the proportion right matters more than getting it fast.
3. **Crating** — draw a light box around the object first, then sketch the real shape inside it. Don't erase the box lines afterwards — they show how you got there.

<CratingDiagram />

Practise on your warm-up sheet: lines, ellipses, and three crated objects — a mug, a stapler, a door handle. Aim for controlled lines, not perfect ones.

<Activity title="The emergency, the brief, the first lines" time="90 min (W session)" grouping="Groups of 4 for the scenario; folder work individual" materials={["Project brief sheet", "A4 folder page", "Pencil and ruler", "Brief-vs-specification sorting cards", "Timer visible to the room"]}>

**The sixty-second emergency — groups of 4, 12 min:** In your group, you have sixty seconds to list everything that has to happen for a firefighter to get through a locked door of a burning building. Sixty seconds, no discussion time, just write. Then read the lists out. Between you the room will have named the whole problem — heat, time, the occupant inside, the fact that nobody is there to turn a handle — before anyone has explained anything to you.

**Sort the deck — groups of 4, 12 min:** Twelve cards, each carrying a statement about the door system. Race to sort them into **brief** and **specification**. First team done reads their sort aloud and the room challenges any card it disagrees with. The distinction — purpose versus measurable requirement — is examined every year, and arguing about a card is how it sticks.

**User and context statement — individual, 15 min:** In your project folder, page 1: who is the occupant, who is the firefighter, and what does this emergency actually demand of the door? Write it as prose, naming both people. A door designed for nobody in particular is a door that fails the C1 criterion.

**One-sentence brief — individual, then swap in pairs, 15 min:** Use the frame: "I am designing a [door system] for a [building façade] that [opens automatically when temperature exceeds a threshold] so that [a firefighter can enter without manual operation]." Then swap folders with a partner and play **spot the specification** — circle anything in their brief that is really a measurable requirement in disguise. Hand it back and fix yours.

**Line games — individual, scored by you, 20 min:** Three timed challenges on your warm-up sheet, scored by you. **Twenty lines in sixty seconds**, single strokes, any feathered line disqualified. **Ellipse darts** — a dot, then five ellipses around it, a point for each closed curve that misses the dot. **Box sprint** — three crated objects in eight minutes. Speed is the cure for a shaky line, and a timer produces speed better than being told to relax.

**Term goal card — individual, 8 min:** One personal goal for the term, plus one strength you bring and one thing you want help with. It goes on the inside cover of your folder where you will actually see it.

**Set the homework hunt — whole class, 8 min:** You are looking for one real sensor-activated system between now and next week. Photograph it. Be ready to name the sensor, the output device, and the trigger.
</Activity>

<Support title="Writing scaffolds">
**User/context sentence starter:** "The [user] needs [product] to [action] because [situation], which means the design must [requirement]."

**Design brief frame:** "I am designing a \_\_\_\_ for a \_\_\_\_ that \_\_\_\_ so that \_\_\_\_."

**For sketching:** Use a grid or pre-printed dotted paper if freehand proportion is difficult. The grid is a scaffold, not a crutch — try without it once you feel more confident.
</Support>
