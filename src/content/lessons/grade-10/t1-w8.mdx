The circuit is more than wires and components: it is a set of precise decisions. Too much current burns the LED. Too little base current and the transistor never switches on. Today you learn the calculation behind each decision, add the thermistor that senses heat, and wire the voltage divider that feeds the transistor, so that by the end of the lesson warming the thermistor in your hand starts the motor.

<Callout type="exam" title="Show every step in a calculation" id="why">
  Paper 1 Section A sometimes includes a resistor calculation, and regularly asks: "Describe how an NTC thermistor responds to a rise in temperature." Examiners award marks for each correct step, not just the final answer: write the formula, substitute the values, calculate, and state the unit. For the thermistor, the answer must use the phrase **Negative Temperature Coefficient** and state that resistance **decreases** as temperature **increases**. Answers that say resistance increases, or omit the direction of change, earn zero.
</Callout>

1. **Guess the resistor — in pairs, 8 min.** Five resistors on the bench, colour bands showing. Read the bands and predict each value, write it down, then measure with the multimeter and score yourselves. The colour code is worth knowing and this is the fastest way to learn it.

2. **Ohm's Law, measured before it is calculated — in pairs, 17 min.** Build a simple resistor-and-battery circuit. Measure the voltage across the resistor and the current through it. Calculate $R = V/I$ from your own two readings and compare with the measured resistance. They will agree to within a few per cent. You have now verified Ohm's Law rather than been told it.

3. **One LED resistor calculation — individual, 10 min.** Supply 9 V, LED forward voltage 2 V, LED current 20 mA, or the values your teacher gives your pair. Every step shown: formula, convert mA to A, substitute, calculate, choose the nearest standard value. Then check it against the worked example.

4. **Prove the thermistor does what it claims — in pairs, 10 min.** Before building anything, measure the thermistor's resistance with the multimeter at room temperature. Hold it in a cup of warm water and measure again. Then iced water. Record all three. You will watch the resistance *fall* as temperature rises.

5. **Draw it, then build it — individual drawing, then in pairs, 30 min.** In the first 8 minutes, draw the Firefighter Barbie circuit from your own understanding: thermistor voltage divider feeding the base through a base resistor, NPN transistor, motor at the collector. Label base, collector and emitter, the current direction, and the IPO roles. Then find the three legs on a real transistor and identify which is which from the datasheet pinout — getting this wrong is the single most common reason a circuit does nothing. Build from your own diagram, not your neighbour's. No soldering.

6. **Trigger-temperature challenge — in pairs, 10 min.** Warm the thermistor and find the temperature at which your motor starts. Record it with the thermometer. If time allows, change the fixed resistor from 10 kΩ to 47 kΩ and find the new trigger temperature. Post your results to the class board.

7. **Wrap — whole class, 5 min.** Pack down, components counted back in, boards cleared. Hand out the installation guide and the two-sheet mechanism planning template: they are the home task for next time.
</Activity>

<Section title="Ohm's Law and the current-limiting resistor" icon="🔧" id="ohms-law">

<Notebook title="Ohm's Law">

Three forms — one formula, three rearrangements:

- $V = I \times R$ (find voltage if you know current and resistance)
- $I = \dfrac{V}{R}$ (find current if you know voltage and resistance)
- $R = \dfrac{V}{I}$ (find resistance if you know voltage and current) ← most used in this project

</Notebook>

### Calculating a current-limiting resistor for an LED

An LED has two critical values:
- **Forward voltage (VLED):** the voltage it drops when operating (typically 2 V for red/green, 3.5 V for blue/white).
- **Forward current (ILED):** the current it needs to operate at full brightness (typically 20 mA = 0.020 A).

The resistor must drop the remaining voltage above the LED's forward voltage:

<Notebook title="Current-limiting resistor">

$$R = \dfrac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}$$

After calculating R, always choose the **nearest standard resistor value above** the calculated value. Standard values follow the E12 or E24 series (common values: 100, 120, 150, 180, 220, 270, 330, 390, 470, 560, 680, 820, 1000 Ω...).

</Notebook>

</Section>

**Given:** Supply voltage = 9 V. LED forward voltage = 2 V. LED current = 20 mA.

**Step 1 — Convert mA to A** before substituting: $20\ \text{mA} = 0.020\ \text{A}$.

**Step 2 — Formula, substitution, result:**

<Formula
  name="Current-limiting resistor"
  equation="R = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "R", meaning: "the resistance of the current-limiting resistor fitted in series with the LED", unit: "Ω" },
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{supply}}", meaning: "the battery voltage feeding the circuit", unit: "V" },
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{LED}}", meaning: "the forward voltage the LED itself drops — about 2 V for red and green, 3.0–3.5 V for blue and white. Subtracted because the LED has already used that much of the supply", unit: "V" },

  ]}

  result="R = 350\ \Omega"
/>

**Step 3 — Choose nearest standard value above 350 Ω:**
From the E12 series: 390 Ω is the next value above 350 Ω.

**Answer:** Use a 390 Ω resistor. This keeps the LED current just below 20 mA (safe), with no risk of burning the LED.
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="The NTC thermistor and the voltage divider" icon="🌡️" id="thermistor">

### NTC thermistor behaviour

NTC stands for **Negative Temperature Coefficient** — as temperature rises, resistance falls.

| Temperature | Thermistor resistance | Effect on circuit |
|---|---|---|
| Cold (room temp) | High (e.g. 10,000 Ω) | Low voltage at divider junction — transistor OFF — motor stopped |
| Warm (hand or breath) | Medium (e.g. 3,000 Ω) | Rising voltage at junction |
| Hot (fire conditions) | Low (e.g. 500 Ω) | High voltage at junction — transistor switches ON — motor runs |

### The voltage divider: how it converts resistance to voltage

Connect the thermistor in series with a fixed resistor between the supply (+9 V) and 0 V. Take the output voltage from the junction between the two components:

<Notebook title="Voltage divider">

$$V_{\text{junction}} = V_{\text{supply}} \times \dfrac{R_{\text{fixed}}}{R_{\text{thermistor}} + R_{\text{fixed}}}$$

As temperature rises: Rthermistor decreases → denominator decreases → junction voltage increases → eventually exceeds the base threshold → transistor ON → motor runs.

</Notebook>

**Choosing the fixed resistor:** Match it to approximately the thermistor's resistance at the trigger temperature. A 10 kΩ fixed resistor with a 10 kΩ thermistor gives exactly half the supply voltage at room temperature. As the thermistor warms and its resistance drops below the fixed resistor value, the junction voltage rises.

### The same idea with light

An **LDR (Light Dependent Resistor)** works on the same principle but responds to light: resistance decreases as light increases. The same voltage divider and transistor circuit applies — only the sensing element changes. If you understand the thermistor circuit, you understand the LDR circuit.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**A thermistor is why your kettle switches itself off, why the fridge stops cooling at the right temperature, and why a car dashboard can warn you the engine is overheating.** Anything that has to *know* a temperature rather than just survive one has a sensor like this inside it.

The fire-detection version you are building is real: heat detectors in kitchens use exactly this principle, because a smoke detector over a cooker would be going off constantly. And the voltage divider around it turns up everywhere else too — the volume knob on a speaker is a variable divider, splitting the signal into the share you asked for.
</Callout>

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Tracing the voltage at the divider junction">
**Circuit:** 9 V battery, 10 kΩ fixed resistor in series with a 10 kΩ NTC thermistor.

**At room temperature (thermistor = 10 kΩ):**

<Formula
  name="Voltage divider"
  equation="V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{out}}", meaning: "the voltage at the junction between the two resistors — the signal fed to the transistor base", unit: "V" },
    { symbol: "V_{\\text{in}}", meaning: "the supply voltage across the whole divider, here the 9 V battery", unit: "V" },
    { symbol: "R_1", meaning: "the upper resistor, between the supply and the junction", unit: "Ω" },
    { symbol: "R_2", meaning: "the lower resistor, between the junction and 0 V. Whichever component sits here is the one whose share of the voltage you are measuring", unit: "Ω" },
  ]}
  substitution="V_{\text{out}} = 9\ \text{V} \times \frac{10{,}000\ \Omega}{10{,}000\ \Omega + 10{,}000\ \Omega}"
  result="V_{\text{out}} = 4.5\ \text{V}"
/>

Transistor base threshold ≈ 0.7 V — but 4.5 V is much higher than 0.7 V, so why isn't the transistor already on? Because there is a **base resistor** (typically 10–47 kΩ) between the junction and the base, which limits base current so the transistor stays in the linear region rather than saturating at room temperature.

**At elevated temperature (thermistor = 1 kΩ):**

<Formula
  name="Voltage divider"
  equation="V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}"
  substitution="V_{\text{out}} = 9\ \text{V} \times \frac{10{,}000\ \Omega}{1{,}000\ \Omega + 10{,}000\ \Omega}"
  result="V_{\text{out}} = 8.2\ \text{V}"
  note="The transistor is now fully saturated — motor at full speed."
/>

In practice, the trigger point is set by the value of the base resistor and the transistor's gain. For this project, hand warmth (≈35°C) should be sufficient to trigger the motor.
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="The NPN transistor as a switch" icon="🔧" id="transistor">

### The three terminals

- **Base (B):** The control input. A small current into the base switches the transistor on.
- **Collector (C):** Connected to the positive supply via the output device (motor or LED). When the transistor is on, current flows from collector to emitter through the output device.
- **Emitter (E):** Connected to 0 V (the negative rail). Current exits the transistor here.

<Notebook title="Transistor switching action">

- **Transistor OFF (base current = 0):** No path from collector to emitter. The motor receives no current and stops.
- **Transistor ON (base current flows):** The transistor conducts from collector to emitter. Current flows through the motor and the motor runs.

**Key point:** A very small base current (microamps) controls a much larger collector current (milliamps or more). The transistor amplifies the control signal.

</Notebook>

<Support title="Scaffolds for calculations, the transistor circuit and troubleshooting">
**Formula strip** — keep on your desk: $V = IR$ · $I = \frac{V}{R}$ · $R = \frac{V}{I}$.
For the LED resistor, always use $R = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}$, not plain $\frac{V}{I}$.

**Step-by-step calculation guide:** Formula → units conversion → substitution → arithmetic → standard value selection. Follow these five steps in order. Never skip the unit conversion step (mA to A is the most common error).

**Units card:** mA to A: divide by 1,000 (20 mA = 0.020 A). kΩ to Ω: multiply by 1,000 (4.7 kΩ = 4,700 Ω).

**Partially completed transistor diagram:** If the full circuit drawing is too open, use the version with the transistor symbol and supply connections already drawn — add the base resistor, the base connection from the voltage divider, the motor symbol, and all labels.

**Multimeter setup for Ohm's Law:** voltage is measured *across* the resistor with the meter in parallel; current is measured *through* it with the meter in series, which means breaking the circuit to insert the meter. Show this once on the visualiser — the series/parallel distinction for meters is the part that is genuinely counter-intuitive.

**Expect a few per cent disagreement** between calculated and measured resistance. Say so in advance, or the first pair to find it will assume they have done something wrong. Tolerance bands on cheap resistors are typically 5%.

**Transistor pinout:** BD139 and TIP120 are not pin-compatible with the small-signal transistors students may have seen. Print the pinout for the exact part in the drawer and put it on the bench — a transistor in backwards is the single most common cause of a dead circuit.

**Blowing the LED is a demonstration, not an accident.** Do it once, deliberately, with a 47 Ω resistor and eye protection, and make the point that the calculation is what stands between a working product and that.

**Thermistor resistance readings to expect:** roughly 10 kΩ at room temperature, falling to around 4–5 kΩ in warm water and rising well above 20 kΩ in iced water. Warm water, not boiling: hand-hot is plenty and keeps the thermometer readings in a sensible range.

**Motor does not run at all:** check the battery rail polarity first, then the transistor orientation against the printed pinout, then that the motor is on the collector and not across the supply.

**Motor runs constantly, even cold:** the base is being held on. Increase the base resistor or the fixed resistor — moving from 10 kΩ to 47 kΩ raises the trigger threshold, which is exactly the change made deliberately in the trigger-temperature challenge.

**Motor flickers or cuts out:** thermistor legs are not seated in the breadboard. Intermittent contact looks like a mysterious fault and is almost always this.

**Breadboard guide:** holes in the same short row on each side of the central channel are connected. The two long rails run the full length of the board.
</Support>
