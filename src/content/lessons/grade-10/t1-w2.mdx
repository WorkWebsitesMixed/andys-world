Orthographic drawing is the language of the workshop — it communicates exactly how an object is shaped and how it should be built. This week you learn the conventions, practise them on a given object, and then sharpen your Paper 1 function-point writing: two skills that examiners reward in different parts of the same paper.

<Callout type="exam" title="Annotation earns marks; vague words don't" id="why">
  Every annotation on Paper 1 must do one of four things: name a **material** with a reason, describe a **join** with a reason, state a **function** with a reason, or give a **dimension** with a reason. Writing "strong" or "good material" earns zero — the mark scheme needs specifics.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="How this session runs">
Each part of the session says whether it is **individual, in pairs, or in a group of 4**, both here and in the section it belongs to. Everything in a bordered table or a `Formula` box goes into your notebook — those are the definitions and equations you are examined on.
</Callout>

<Section title="Orthographic drawing: the rules" icon="✏️" id="orthographic">

### Two conventions in use worldwide

- **First-angle projection** (common in Europe): the front view is in the top-left, the side view is to its right showing the side you see from the left.
- **Third-angle projection** (common in North America): views are placed on the same side as the direction you look from.

Both are acceptable on IGCSE drawings; always include the projection symbol so the reader knows which you used.

### Line types — why each exists

| Line | Appearance | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Outline | Thick continuous | The visible edges of the object |
| Hidden detail | Thin dashed | Edges that exist but are hidden by a nearer face |
| Centre line | Thin chain (dash-dot) | Axes of symmetry, centre of circles |
| Dimension line | Thin continuous + arrowheads | Measurements; never crosses an outline |

### Mark-worthy annotation — the four categories

A single annotation can earn a mark if it states: **material** (what it is made of), **join** (how two parts connect), **function** (what a feature does), or **dimension** (a size with a reason). Each must include a reason to earn the mark.

Examples on a shelf bracket:
- "3 mm mild steel — resists bending under shelf load" (material + reason)
- "Riveted butt joint — permanent, no welding equipment needed" (join + reason)
- "Pivot pin — allows 90° fold for flat-pack transport" (function + reason)

<Callout type="tip" title="Blind describe-and-draw · 20 min · in pairs">
Back to back. One of you takes an object from the box and describes it in words only — no gestures, no naming it. The other draws front and side views from that description alone. Then reveal, compare, and swap.

Within ninety seconds you will discover that "it's about this big" is useless and "the base is 60 mm wide and 20 mm deep" is not. That discovery is the whole reason orthographic conventions exist — you are inventing them before being taught them.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Orthographic views of a shelf bracket">
**Object:** A simple L-shaped wall bracket with two mounting holes.

**Front view:** Shows the L-shape outline in thick lines. The mounting holes are shown as circles (outlines). Hidden edges behind the bracket are shown as dashed lines.

**Side view:** Placed directly to the right of the front view (or to the left in first-angle). Shows the depth of the bracket as a rectangle.

**Four annotations:**
1. "18 mm mild steel plate — high tensile strength for heavy loads" (material)
2. "Countersunk M5 screws — flush surface, no snagging hazard" (join)
3. "Angled rib — prevents the bracket twisting under a lateral load" (function)
4. "50 mm arm length — matches standard shelf depth" (dimension with reason)

<Section title="Exam practice — Paper 1 function points" icon="📝" id="function-points">

### What Paper 1 part (a) asks

Part (a) gives you an everyday product and asks for four function points — specific statements of what the product must do. The mark scheme rejects:
- Generic words: "strong", "comfortable", "easy to use"
- Aesthetic descriptions: "looks nice", "modern design"
- Material names without a function: "made of plastic"

### The function-point formula

"The [product] must [specific action] so that [user-centred reason]."

Example for a phone stand:
- **Weak:** "The stand must hold the phone."
- **Strong:** "The stand must support a 200 g phone at 70° from horizontal without tipping, so the user can view the screen hands-free while cooking."

### Section A quick-fire

Five short Section A items test your recall of the design process (Week 1 content). Answer in one or two sentences — no wasted words. Rewrite any answer that contains a generic word.

<Callout type="tip" title="The loophole game · inside the function-point slot · in pairs">
Hand your four function points to a partner. Their job is to describe the worst possible product that still passes all four. Every loophole is a point to them and a rewrite for you.

"Must be easy to use" dies instantly. "Must open fully with one hand in under two seconds" survives.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Activity title="Blind drawing, line types, and function points" time="90 min (W session) + 45 min (B session)" grouping="Pairs for the drawing games; folder work individual" materials={["A3 drawing paper", "Pencil, ruler, set square", "Given product for function-point practice", "A box of hidden objects for the blind-drawing game", "Screen or folder to hide objects behind", "Project folder"]}>

**W session:**

1. **Blind describe-and-draw — in pairs, 20 min.** In pairs, back to back. One of you takes an object from the box and describes it in words only — no gestures, no naming it. The other draws front and side views from that description alone. Then reveal, compare, and swap. You will discover within ninety seconds that "it's about this big" is useless and "the base is 60 mm wide and 20 mm deep" is not. That discovery is the entire reason orthographic conventions exist — you are inventing them before being taught them.

2. **Line-type relay — groups of 4, 10 min.** Groups of 4 at the board. Each runner draws one line type — thick continuous outline, thin dashed hidden detail, thin chain centre line, thin continuous dimension line with arrowheads — and hands the pen on. Wrong line type sends the team back a step. Four correct lines, fastest group wins.

3. **The real thing — individual, 25 min.** Draw front and side views of the given object with correct line types. Check as you go: outlines thick, hidden edges dashed, centre lines chain. Annotate with three mark-worthy notes — one material, one join or construction, one function — each with a reason.

4. **Project folder — individual, 25 min.** Front and side orthographic views of your own door leaf concept. A first attempt, not a final design. Add two mark-worthy annotations: one material, one join or function.

5. **Peer critique — in pairs, 10 min.** Swap with a partner. Find one thing that genuinely works in their drawing and say specifically why, then find one line that is the wrong type. Both halves are required.

**B session:**

6. **Function points, then the loophole game — individual, then in pairs, 25 min.** Write four function points for the given product using the formula. Then hand them to a partner whose job is to describe the worst possible product that still passes all four. Every loophole they find is a point to them and a rewrite for you. "Must be easy to use" dies instantly under this test; "must open fully with one hand in under two seconds" survives.

7. **Project folder — individual, 12 min.** Four function points for the Firefighter Barbie door system itself, written to survive the loophole test. These go straight into next week's specification.

8. **Section A set — individual, 8 min.** Five items on the design process. Self-mark, then rewrite the two weakest, removing every generic word.
</Activity>

<Support title="Scaffolds for drawing and writing">
**Line types reminder card:** Keep it on your desk. Before handing in, check each line in your drawing against the card.

**Annotation checklist:** For each annotation — does it name a material, join, or function? Does it give a reason? If not, rewrite it.

**Function-point frame:** "The [product] must [specific action] so that [reason linked to user or use context]."

**For orthographic:** Use a pre-printed light-grey orthographic grid if you struggle to align the two views. The front and side views must be at exactly the same height.

**Your door leaf isn't final yet:** it's fine if it's just a rectangle with a rough size at this stage — you're practising the drawing convention, not locking in the design. You'll revisit the form itself once your specification (Week 3) and mechanism (Weeks 4–5) are settled.

**Running the blind describe-and-draw:** back to back, or a folder standing between them. Ban three things and it works immediately — no naming the object, no hand gestures, no "you know, like a…". Objects that work best are simple and slightly odd: a stapler, a tape dispenser, a bulldog clip, a padlock. Ninety seconds each way is enough; longer and the energy drops.

**If a pair is stuck on the blind drawing:** prompt the describer with one question — "how wide is it compared to how tall?" That single question converts them from adjectives to proportions, which is the whole lesson.

**Line-type relay at the board:** four line types, four runners. Keep a printed key beside you to adjudicate quickly, because the arguments are the point and they need settling fast.

</Support>
