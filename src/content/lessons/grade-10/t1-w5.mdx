Last week you saw how a crank turns rotary motion into reciprocating movement. Cams do the same job but give you control over the *shape* of the motion — the door can rise slowly, stay open for a fixed period (dwell), then close. This week you choose between crank and cam for your Firefighter Barbie door, justify the choice in writing, and lock in your final mechanism design.

</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Cam profile", definition: "The outline shape of the cam disc. The profile determines the follower's movement pattern (how fast it rises, how long it dwells, how it falls)." },
  { term: "Pear cam", definition: "A cam with a pointed lobe and a circular body. Produces a distinct rise (lobe pushes follower up), dwell (circular section — follower stays at height), and fall (follower drops back)." },
  { term: "Follower", definition: "The component that rests on the cam surface and moves as the cam rotates. Converts the cam's rotation into the output motion." },
  { term: "Dwell", definition: "A period in the cam cycle where the follower height does not change — the cam profile is circular at that point. Used to hold a door open for a fixed time." },
  { term: "Stroke (cam)", definition: "The total rise of the follower from its lowest to its highest position. Determined by the difference between the smallest and largest cam radius." },
]} />

<Section title="Cam profiles and follower types" icon="🔧" id="cams">

### The three cam profiles you need to know

| Profile | Shape | Motion produced | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pear | Rounded lobe + circular body | Rise → dwell → fall | Door that stays open then closes |
| Snail / heart | Constantly changing radius | Gradual continuous rise, rapid fall | Constant-speed rise applications |
| Eccentric | Off-centre circle | Smooth, continuous oscillation | Gentle back-and-forth motion |

### Two follower types

- **Knife-edge follower:** Simple, cheap, follows the cam profile precisely — but wears quickly. Good for light loads, low speed.
- **Roller follower:** A small wheel at the tip rolls against the cam surface. Greatly reduces friction and wear. Recommended for the Firefighter Barbie mechanism where repeated operation is needed.
- **Flat-face follower:** A flat plate rests against the cam. Works with concave cam sections but cannot follow sharp internal profiles.

### Tracing follower motion on a pear cam

As the cam rotates through 360°:
- **Rise:** The lobe comes under the follower and pushes it upward (door opens).
- **Dwell:** The circular section of the cam is under the follower — follower stays at its highest point (door remains open).
- **Fall:** The follower drops back to the body radius (door closes, spring or gravity assisted).

### Crank vs cam — choosing for the project

| Criterion | Crank | Cam |
|---|---|---|
| Predictable stroke | Yes — exactly 2 × arm length | Depends on profile |
| Dwell (door stays open) | Not without extra linkage | Yes — pear cam provides dwell |
| Adjustability | Change arm length | Change cam profile |
| Complexity | Lower — fewer parts | Slightly higher — cam + follower |
| IGCSE exam coverage | Covered in §4.3.2 | Covered in §4.3.1 |
| Motor shaft vs door hinge | Needs extra work — the cradle holds the motor shaft **horizontal**, but the door hinges on a **vertical** axis, so a crank must solve that change of axis itself | Works directly — a cam takes drive straight off the horizontal shaft, no extra linkage needed |

<Callout type="warn" title="Check the geometry before you commit">
  The motor cradle on the façade deck holds the motor shaft **horizontal**. The door hinges on a **vertical** axis (it swings inward, like a real door). A cam converts horizontal rotation into the door's motion with no extra parts. A crank arm, on the other hand, naturally produces motion in the plane it rotates in — which is not the plane the door swings in, so a crank arrangement needs you to design an extra link or mount to bridge the two axes. This is a genuine, open engineering problem — not a hint that crank is the "wrong" answer, just that it costs you more design work if you choose it.
</Callout>

**For the Firefighter Barbie project:** a crank gives a defined, predictable stroke — good for a door that must open a fixed distance. A pear cam gives a dwell period — good if the door must stay open long enough for the firefighter to enter, and it fits the horizontal-shaft cradle without extra linkage. Both are valid choices; your written justification must use these technical reasons, not "I prefer the cam" or "the crank is simpler."

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**The camshaft in a car engine** is a row of pear cams on one rotating shaft, and each one opens a valve at exactly the right moment and holds it open for exactly the right fraction of a revolution. The dwell you struggled to design is the whole reason the engine runs — the valve has to *stay* open long enough for the gases to move.

Smaller and easier to see: the barrel of a music box, the mechanism inside a wind-up toy, and the moving figures in a mechanical automaton. If you have ever seen a wooden automaton where a bird bobs up and down, you have watched a cam profile you could now draw.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Pear cam — tracing the follower">
**Setup:** A pear cam mounted on a motor shaft, with a roller follower pressing against it from above.

**Full rotation trace:**
- 0° – 90°: Lobe rises under the follower. Follower pushes upward. Door pivots open.
- 90° – 180°: Circular dwell section. Follower stays at maximum height. Door stays open.
- 180° – 270°: Lobe passes; follower drops back to body radius. Door closes.
- 270° – 360°: Circular body section. Follower at minimum height. Door fully closed.

**Annotation on the sketch:**
- "Pear cam — mild steel — hardened surface resists wear from roller contact"
- "Roller follower — reduces friction compared to knife-edge — extends cam life"
- "Dwell section — keeps door open for 180° of rotation — approximately 1 second at typical motor speed"
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="Exam practice — annotation and rendering" icon="📝" id="annotation">

### What earns marks in annotation (direct prep for M1)

The Week 6 bonus session (M1) is a timed test of Paper 1 parts (a) and (b). Part (b) requires two annotated sketch methods. This session is your final practice before the test.

Annotation that earns marks must include one of:
- **Material** — name it and give a reason: "mild steel crank arm — strong in tension and compression"
- **Join** — name it and give a reason: "M4 bolt — removable for maintenance without tools"
- **Function** — name the feature's purpose: "connecting rod pivot — allows relative rotation as the crank sweeps"

### Simple rendering for material indication

- **Metal:** Hatching — parallel diagonal lines, evenly spaced.
- **Wood:** Wavy grain lines following the surface direction.
- **Plastic/acrylic:** Light stippling or left blank with a label.

Rendering is not compulsory on Paper 1, but it differentiates good work from excellent work and takes under 30 seconds per surface.

</Section>

**W session:**

1. **Feel before you name — groups of 4, 8 min.** The cam models go round the tables. Turn each one slowly with a finger resting on the follower and describe out loud what your finger does. Up, then nothing for a while, then down. You have just described rise, dwell and fall without being told the words.

2. **Cut your own cam — groups of 4, 32 min.** In groups of 4, cut three cam profiles from cardboard: a circle mounted off-centre (eccentric), a pear, and one of your own design. Mount each on a shaft with a follower resting on it. Turn each through a full revolution and record what the follower does every quarter turn. The pear will give you a long dwell; the eccentric will give you continuous smooth motion and no dwell at all. That contrast is the whole topic.

3. **The design challenge — groups of 4, 20 min.** Now design a cam to a brief: the follower must rise, stay up for roughly a third of a revolution, then fall. Cut it, test it, and adjust. This is genuinely hard on the first attempt and that is fine — record what you changed between attempt one and attempt two, because that record is worth more than a cam that worked first time.

4. **Sketch and label — individual, 15 min.** A pear cam with a roller follower, labelled with rise, dwell and fall on the profile and the follower motion type at each section.

5. **Final mechanism design — individual, 15 min.** Crank or cam for the Firefighter Barbie door — you have now built both, so choose from experience. Sketch it fully annotated: every part named, material stated for each, motion type noted at each link. Write a three-sentence justification with the word "because" in every sentence.

**B session:**

6. **Annotation practice — individual, 25 min.** On a given mechanism sketch, add at least four mark-worthy annotations. Then try rendering one metal surface with hatching.

7. **Annotation referee — in pairs, 20 min.** Swap with a partner and referee theirs in red: does each annotation name a material, join or function, *and* give a reason? Every annotation that fails either test gets circled. Rewrite yours before this goes anywhere near M1 next week.
</Activity>

<Support title="Scaffolds for cam design and annotation">
**Cam comparison table:** A pre-filled table comparing crank and cam across four criteria. Fill in the last column (which is better for the Firefighter Barbie door) — then write your 3-sentence justification from that column.

**Annotation checklist:** (1) Material named? (2) Join type named? (3) Function described? (4) Reason given for each? A complete annotation hits at least two of these four and gives a reason.

**Mechanism labelling exemplar:** A fully labelled exemplar of a crank-and-door sketch is available for reference — do not copy it; use it to check that your labels are at the right level of specificity.

**Cutting cam profiles safely:** cardboard on a cutting mat, craft knife pulled towards nobody, and the shaft hole punched rather than cut. If knives are a concern with this group, pre-cut the eccentric and the pear and let them cut only their own third profile with scissors.

**Making the dwell visible:** the follower has to stay in contact. A skewer follower in a card guide with a small weight or an elastic band holding it down against the cam gives a far clearer rise-dwell-fall than a follower resting loose, which just bounces.

**If nobody's cam produces a dwell:** the profile needs a genuinely circular section, concentric with the shaft, over the part of the rotation where the follower should hold still. Draw that arc with compasses centred on the shaft hole before cutting anything — freehand pear shapes almost never dwell.

**The design challenge is meant to fail first time.** Rise, hold for a third of a turn, fall is hard to hit on attempt one. Say so before they start, and make the record of what changed between attempts the assessed output rather than the cam itself.

</Support>
