Three sheets of the same A4 paper, folded three different ways. One of them will hold a stack of textbooks; one will collapse almost immediately. Same material, same mass — so whatever explains the difference cannot be the paper. That is the idea this whole session is built on, and you will meet it again every time a structure has to carry a load.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
Structures is the theory backbone of the whole course and of your project. <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section B</ExamLink> asks you to classify structures, name forces, and calculate stress and factor of safety — and it awards **method marks** for the formula and the substitution even when the arithmetic goes wrong. Write every stage down, always.
</Callout>

Each idea comes in the same order: **a quick test, then the notes that explain it.** Everything in a bordered table or a `Formula` box goes into your notebook — those are the definitions and equations you are examined on. The tests are there so the notes mean something when you read them back.
</Callout>

  { term: "Shell structure", definition: "A structure where the outer skin carries the load. The surface itself is the structural element — there is no internal frame. Examples: egg, car body panel, dome, tin can." },
  { term: "Mass structure", definition: "A solid structure where the whole volume of material is under compression. No internal cavity. Examples: dam wall, concrete foundation, brick wall, cliff." },

  { term: "Neutral axis", definition: "The layer inside a bent beam where the material is neither stretched nor squashed, and the bending stress is zero. It separates the compression face from the tension face." },

### Frame, shell, and mass

Copy this table.

| Type | How load is carried | Key feature | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | Members and joints | Spaces between members carry nothing | Bicycle frame, roof truss, your project |
| Shell | Outer skin | Surface is the structural element | Egg, car bonnet, dome |
| Mass | Entire volume | All material in compression | Dam, brick wall, concrete foundation |

**Your paper columns were shells.** The load was carried by the skin of the tube, and there was nothing inside. That is exactly why the shape mattered so much: in a shell, the geometry of the surface *is* the structure.

**Exam trap:** A cardboard box is a shell structure, not a frame. The faces carry the load. A box made of rods glued at the corners is a frame — the rods carry the load, the spaces do not.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
Walk out of school and you can classify almost everything you pass. The **frames**: a bicycle, scaffolding, an electricity pylon, the roof timbers of a house, a shopping trolley. The **shells**: a car body panel, a drinks can, a bike helmet, an aircraft fuselage, a plastic chair. The **mass**: a kerbstone, a brick wall, a dam, the concrete base a lamppost stands in.

Where it gets interesting is the things that changed. Early cars had a separate steel frame with a body bolted on; almost every modern car is a shell, where the body panels themselves carry the load. That change made cars lighter and safer at the same time — the same amount of steel doing a better job because of where it was put.
</Callout>

### Triangulation: why triangles are rigid

A triangle cannot be deformed without changing the length of one of its sides. A square frame can be **racked** — pushed into a parallelogram — without any side changing length at all. Adding one diagonal converts the square into two triangles, and the racking stops dead.

<Callout type="tip" title="Watch the board demo · 3 min · whole class">
A square frame pushed over, then the same square with one diagonal added. Notice what happens to the corner-to-corner distance when the square racks — that is the length the diagonal refuses to allow to change.
</Callout>

This is why trusses, bicycle frames, scaffolding and roof structures are all triangulated. It is also the cheapest structural upgrade that exists: one extra member, no extra material thickness.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
Look at any **garden gate**. There is a diagonal brace running corner to corner, and it always runs *up* towards the hinge side. Without it the gate slowly racks into a parallelogram and starts catching on the ground — which is exactly the failure you can reproduce with a square frame in ten seconds.

The same triangle is in the arm of a crane, the side of an electricity pylon, the frame of a bunk bed, the back of a folding chair, and the roof space above your head. Once you have seen it, scaffolding stops looking like a random pile of poles.
</Callout>

### The five forces

Copy this table — all five, with the example.

| Force | Direction | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tension | Pulls apart along the axis | Stretches the member | Bottom chord of a truss, guy wire |
| Compression | Pushes together along the axis | Squashes the member | Column, strut, concrete wall |
| Shear | Acts across the member | Cuts or slides layers | Bolt through a joint, scissors |
| Torsion | Twists around the axis | Rotates the cross-section | Driveshaft, screwdriver bit |
| Bending | Combination of tension + compression | Curves the member | Loaded beam |

Your paper columns failed in **compression** — specifically by buckling, which is what a slender member in compression does before it ever gets close to being crushed.

### Seeing bending, with a book

Bending is the force most often got wrong in the exam, because it is really two forces at once.

<Callout type="tip" title="Test it now · 4 min · in pairs">
Take a thick paperback or a fat stack of paper and bend it into a curve. Watch the page edges at the ends of the block.

- The pages on the **inside** of the curve are pushed together — that face is in **compression**.
- The pages on the **outside** slide forward and are stretched apart — that face is in **tension**.
- Somewhere in the middle, one layer does neither. That is the **neutral axis**, where bending stress is zero.
</Callout>

This is why an I-beam has flanges at the top and bottom: material near the neutral axis is barely working, so it is moved out to where the stress is highest. Same material, better geometry, far stiffer beam.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Floor joists are always on edge, never flat.** Same plank, same timber, turned through 90° — and it carries several times more load, because turning it moves the material away from the neutral axis. Try it with a ruler: bend it flat between two hands, then bend it on edge. It is the same ruler.

This is also why steel beams over doorways and in building sites are I-shaped rather than solid rectangles: the middle of a beam is doing almost nothing, so the steel is taken out of there and put in the flanges where the stress is. Less material, stiffer beam, cheaper building.
</Callout>

<Section title="Stress" icon="📐" id="stress">

Copy this formula.

<Formula
  name="Stress"
  equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}"
  substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{mm}^2}"
  result="\text{Stress} = 10\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 10\ \text{MPa}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "\\text{Stress}", meaning: "how hard the material itself is working — the share of the load carried by each square millimetre of cross-section", unit: "N/mm² (= MPa)" },
    { symbol: "\\text{Force}", meaning: "the load pushing or pulling on the member, converted from mass by multiplying kilograms by about 10", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "\\text{Area}", meaning: "the cross-sectional area carrying that load — the area of the cut face if you sliced the member through", unit: "mm²" },
  ]}
  note="A force of 500 N acting on a cross-section of 50 mm². Note that N/mm² and MPa are the same unit."
/>

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Stiletto heels and snowshoes.** The same person, the same weight — but a stiletto puts it through roughly 1 cm² and dents a wooden floor, while a snowshoe spreads it over about 500 cm² and stays on top of the snow. The force never changed; only the area did.

Same idea everywhere else: a rucksack with wide straps is comfortable and one with thin cords cuts into your shoulders. A drawing pin is sharp at one end so a small hand force becomes enormous stress at the point, and flat at the other so the same force stays comfortable on your thumb.
</Callout>

A thicker cross-section reduces stress for the same force. This is why structural members get wider at points of high load — and it is part of why the paper tube outperformed the flat-folded strip.

**Converting mass to force:** loads are usually given as a mass in kg, but structures are calculated in newtons. Multiply by roughly 10 (strictly 9.81). A 2 kg load is about 20 N.

<Callout type="tip" title="Apply it to your own column · 8 min · individual, using your group's result">
Take the column that held the most. Estimate the cross-sectional area of the **paper itself** — not the area of the circle it encloses. For a cylinder: measure the circumference in mm and multiply by the paper thickness (about 0.1 mm). Then convert the mass of books it held into newtons, and calculate the stress in the paper at the moment it failed.

Your answer will be an estimate, and that is fine. An estimate you have stated clearly earns marks; a blank earns none.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section title="Factor of Safety" icon="🛟" id="fos">

Copy this formula and the three rearrangements.

<Formula
  name="Factor of safety"
  equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
  substitution="\text{FoS} = \frac{200\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{N}}"
  result="\text{FoS} = 4.0"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "\\text{FoS}", meaning: "how many times over the structure could carry its intended load before it fails — a pure ratio, so it never carries a unit", unit: "no unit" },
    { symbol: "\\text{Failure Load}", meaning: "the load at which the structure actually breaks or permanently deforms. Found by testing one to destruction, or from published material data — never a guess", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "\\text{Working Load}", meaning: "the largest load the structure is designed to carry in normal use, decided by you from what the user will really put on it", unit: "N" },
  ]}
  note="A shelf bracket that permanently deforms at 200 N, designed to carry 50 N, could take four times its working load before failing."
/>

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Every lift you have ever been in.** The plate by the doors says something like "8 persons / 630 kg" — that is a working load. The cables and brake are built to hold several times it, and lift codes typically demand a FoS of around 10 or higher, because the consequence of failure is people.

You will also find it on climbing rope (rated far above any climber's weight, because a fall loads it many times harder than hanging does), on the ladder in your garage, on a car jack, and on the weight limit sign at the entrance to a bridge or a car park. Every one of those numbers is a failure load divided by a factor somebody chose deliberately.
</Callout>

- $\text{FoS} = \dfrac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}$
- $\text{Working Load} = \dfrac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{FoS}}$
- $\text{Failure Load} = \text{Working Load} \times \text{FoS}$

FoS is a **ratio**, so it has no unit — writing "FoS = 4.0 N" is wrong. Because it is a ratio, both loads only have to be in the *same* unit, so kg works as well as N.

For structures that affect human safety — buildings, bridges, lifting equipment — FoS values of 2.5 to 6 are typical. Aircraft run much lower, around 1.5, because every kilogram costs fuel; that is why aircraft parts are inspected and replaced on a fixed schedule instead.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Three calculations in sequence">

1. **FoS.** A shelf is designed to hold 30 kg (approximately 300 N). Testing shows it fails at 1,200 N.

    <Formula
      equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
      substitution="\text{FoS} = \frac{1200\ \text{N}}{300\ \text{N}}"
      result="\text{FoS} = 4.0"
      note="Adequate for a domestic shelf — a comfortable safety margin."
    />

2. **Working load from a required FoS.** The same design must be re-rated for a school, where a FoS of 6 is required.

    <Formula
      equation="\text{Working Load} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{FoS}}"
      substitution="\text{Working Load} = \frac{1200\ \text{N}}{6}"
      result="\text{Working Load} = 200\ \text{N}"
      note="Roughly 20 kg. Demanding a higher factor of safety does not change the shelf — it lowers what you are allowed to put on it."
    />

3. **Stress.** The vertical support of the bracket is 25 mm × 25 mm square timber, carrying 300 N in compression.

    <Formula
      equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}"
      substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{300\ \text{N}}{25 \times 25\ \text{mm}^2} = \frac{300}{625}"
      result="\text{Stress} = 0.48\ \text{N/mm}^2"
      note="Well within timber's compressive strength of about 30 N/mm² — this member is nowhere near its limit, so if the shelf fails it will fail somewhere else."
    />

</WorkedExample>

<Support title="If you get stuck">

**Structure classification — if uncertain:** Ask "does the skin or surface carry the load, or do separate members carry it?" If separate members (rods, beams, struts), it is a frame. If the surface itself carries the load, it is a shell. If it is solid throughout with all the material in compression, it is a mass.

**Converting mass to force:** multiply kilograms by 10 to get newtons (strictly 9.81, but 10 is accepted and faster). 1.85 kg becomes 18.5 N. Always write the unit — an answer with no unit loses the mark even when the number is right.

**Estimating the area of a paper column:** you need the area of the *paper*, not the area it encloses. For a cylinder, measure the circumference in mm and multiply by the paper thickness, about 0.1 mm for ordinary A4. A 210 mm circumference gives roughly 21 mm². State your assumption on the page.

**"My stress answer looks like a strange number."** It probably is, and that is fine — paper is thin, so the areas are tiny and the stresses come out high. Marks here are for the method, not for landing on a textbook value.

**"My columns all collapsed instantly."** Check the height is the same for all three and that the tape is holding the shape closed. A tube that springs open is not a tube. Load books one at a time and centrally — a book placed off-centre topples the column instead of buckling it.

**Nothing has failed yet and time is running out:** stop, record the number of books you reached, and write "did not fail at 6 books — test stopped". A lower bound is usable data.

**Which formula do I need?** If the question gives you two loads and asks how safe it is, that is FoS. If it gives you a force and an area, that is stress. If it gives you a FoS and asks what it can carry, rearrange to Working Load = Failure Load ÷ FoS.

</Support>
