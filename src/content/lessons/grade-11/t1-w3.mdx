Today nothing gets explained to you before it gets tested. You will crush an egg, buckle a paper column, bend a foam beam until you can see the forces in it, and then build a bracket out of spaghetti and load it until it snaps. The number your bracket fails at is the number you will do the maths on — so break it honestly.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
Structures is the theory backbone of the whole course and of your project. <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section B</ExamLink> asks you to classify structures, name forces, and calculate stress and factor of safety — and it awards **method marks** for the formula and the substitution even when the arithmetic goes wrong. <KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Frame structure", definition: "A structure made of individual members (rods, beams, or struts) connected at joints. The members carry the load; the spaces between them carry nothing. Examples: bicycle frame, roof truss, shelf bracket, Eiffel Tower." },
  { term: "Shell structure", definition: "A structure where the outer skin carries the load. The surface itself is the structural element — there is no internal frame. Examples: egg, car body panel, dome, tin can." },
  { term: "Mass structure", definition: "A solid structure where the whole volume of material is under compression. No internal cavity. Examples: dam wall, concrete foundation, brick wall, cliff." },
  { term: "Triangulation", definition: "Adding a diagonal member to a square frame to convert it into two triangles. A triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of one of its sides, so a triangulated frame cannot rack." },
  { term: "Stress", definition: "Force divided by the cross-sectional area carrying it. Measured in N/mm² (identical to MPa). Two members carrying the same load can be under completely different stress if their cross-sections differ." },
  { term: "Factor of Safety (FoS)", definition: "The ratio of the load at which a structure fails to the maximum load it is designed to carry in use. FoS = Failure Load divided by Working Load. A FoS of 1 means the structure fails exactly at its working load — far too risky. Structural engineering uses 2 to 6 or higher." },
  { term: "Strut", definition: "A structural member under compression. It pushes back against the load trying to squash it. Columns and vertical posts are typically struts." },
  { term: "Tie", definition: "A structural member under tension. It resists being pulled apart. The bottom chord of a roof truss is typically a tie." },
]} />

<Section title="Types of structure" icon="🏗️" id="types">

### Frame, shell, and mass

| Type | How load is carried | Key feature | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | Members and joints | Spaces between members carry nothing | Bicycle frame, roof truss, your project |
| Shell | Outer skin | Surface is the structural element | Egg, car bonnet, dome |
| Mass | Entire volume | All material in compression | Dam, brick wall, concrete foundation |

**Exam trap:** A cardboard box is a shell structure, not a frame. The faces carry the load. A box made of straws glued at the corners is a frame — the straws carry the load, the spaces do not.

### The egg test, and why it works

An egg is a shell structure. Squeezed end-to-end in a closed palm, an intact egg is extremely hard to break — the curved shell spreads your grip into pure compression, and eggshell is very strong in compression. Poke the same egg once with a fingertip at the side and it fails instantly, because a point load bends the shell, and eggshell is weak in bending.

That single contrast is the whole idea of a shell: **the geometry decides which force the material has to face.** Change the geometry and you change the force, without changing the material at all.

### Triangulation: why triangles are rigid

A triangle cannot be deformed without changing the length of one of its sides. A square frame can be **racked** — pushed into a parallelogram — without any side changing length at all. Adding one diagonal converts the square into two triangles, and the racking stops dead.

This is why trusses, bicycle frames, scaffolding and roof structures are all triangulated. It is also the cheapest structural upgrade that exists: one extra member, no extra material thickness.

<Section title="Forces on structures" icon="⚡" id="forces">

### The five forces

| Force | Direction | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tension | Pulls apart along the axis | Stretches the member | Bottom chord of a truss, guy wire |
| Compression | Pushes together along the axis | Squashes the member | Column, strut, concrete wall |
| Shear | Acts across the member | Cuts or slides layers | Bolt through a joint, scissors |
| Torsion | Twists around the axis | Rotates the cross-section | Driveshaft, screwdriver bit |
| Bending | Combination of tension + compression | Curves the member | Loaded beam |

### Seeing bending with your own eyes

Bending is the force students most often get wrong in the exam, because it is really two forces at once. Station 3 makes it visible: draw a straight line along the side of a foam block, then bend the block into a curve.

- The line on the **top** face gets **shorter** — that face is in **compression**.
- The line on the **bottom** face gets **longer** — that face is in **tension**.
- Somewhere in the middle the line does not change length at all. That layer is the **neutral axis**, where bending stress is zero.

This is why an I-beam has flanges at the top and bottom: material near the neutral axis is barely working, so it is moved out to where the stress is highest. Same material, better geometry, far stiffer beam.

<Section title="Stress and Factor of Safety" icon="📐" id="stress-fos">

### Stress

<Formula
  name="Stress"
  equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}"
  substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{mm}^2}"
  result="\text{Stress} = 10\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 10\ \text{MPa}"
  note="A force of 500 N acting on a cross-section of 50 mm². Note that N/mm² and MPa are the same unit."
/>

A thicker cross-section reduces stress for the same force. This is why structural members get wider at points of high load — and why the paper column in Station 2 survives so much more load as a tube than as a folded strip.

### Factor of Safety

**Example:** A shelf bracket permanently deforms at 200 N. It is designed to carry a maximum load of 50 N.

<Formula
  name="Factor of safety"
  equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
  substitution="\text{FoS} = \frac{200\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{N}}"
  result="\text{FoS} = 4.0"
  note="The bracket could carry four times its working load before failing."
/>

For structures that affect human safety — buildings, bridges, lifting equipment — FoS values of 2.5 to 6 are typical. Aircraft run much lower (around 1.5) because every kilogram costs fuel, which is why aircraft parts are inspected and replaced on a schedule instead.

**Converting mass to force:** loads are usually given as a mass in kg, but structures are calculated in newtons. Multiply by roughly 10 (strictly 9.81). A 2 kg load is about 20 N.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="From a broken bracket to a judgement">

This is exactly the sequence you will run on your own bracket in Phase 4.

1. **Record the failure load.** The bracket held a cup that was filled with washers until it snapped. Final measured mass: 1.85 kg. Convert to force — $1.85 \times 10 \approx 18.5\ \text{N}$. That is the **failure load**.

2. **State the working load.** The user this bracket is for wants to hang a 600 g bag from it. $0.6 \times 10 = 6\ \text{N}$. That is the **working load**.

3. **Calculate the factor of safety.**

    <Formula
      equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
      substitution="\text{FoS} = \frac{18.5\ \text{N}}{6\ \text{N}}"
      result="\text{FoS} = 3.1"
    />

4. **Calculate the stress in the member that actually broke.** It was a spaghetti strut, four strands taped together, each strand about 1.8 mm diameter. Area of one strand is $\pi r^2 = \pi \times 0.9^2 \approx 2.5\ \text{mm}^2$, so four strands give about $10\ \text{mm}^2$.

    <Formula
      equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}"
      substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{18.5\ \text{N}}{10\ \text{mm}^2}"
      result="\text{Stress} = 1.85\ \text{N/mm}^2"
    />

5. **Make the judgement.** A FoS of 3.1 is acceptable for a domestic product carrying a non-critical load. But note *where* it failed: if it failed at a **joint** rather than in a **member**, the FoS above describes the tape, not the spaghetti — and the honest design conclusion is "strengthen the joints", not "use more material".

</WorkedExample>

<Activity title="Predict, break, measure, calculate" time="90 min" grouping="Teams of 3–4; calculations written individually" materials={["Raw egg (teacher demo) + hand sanitiser", "Drinking straws and masking tape", "A4 paper and sticky tape", "Foam block or dense sponge with a line drawn along one side", "Dried spaghetti", "Plastic cup, string, washers or coins", "Digital kitchen scale", "Ruler and calculator", "Results sheet"]}>

**Every phase starts with a prediction.** Write your guess down *before* the test and circle it. You are not marked on being right — you are marked on having committed.

**Hook — the egg that will not break (0–8 min):** Predict how many of you can crush a raw egg end-to-end in a closed palm. Watch the demo. Then watch a single fingertip at the side of the shell do what a whole fist could not. Write one sentence: which force was the shell strong against, and which was it weak against?

**Station 1 — racking (8–15 min):** Build a square from four straws taped at the corners. Push one corner sideways. Record what happens to the shape and to the side lengths. Now tape one diagonal straw across it and push again. Write the one-sentence rule you have just discovered.

**Station 2 — the paper column (15–22 min):** You get three sheets of A4. Make three columns of the same height: one rolled into a cylinder, one folded into a triangular tube, one folded into a square tube. Predict the ranking before you load them. Stack textbooks on each until it buckles and record the number of books each held. Same material, same mass — different geometry.

**Station 3 — seeing the neutral axis (22–30 min):** Take the foam block with the line drawn along its side. Bend it into a curve. Watch the line: shorter at the top, longer at the bottom, unchanged in the middle. Sketch the block bent, and label compression, tension and the neutral axis on your sketch.

**Build (30–52 min):** In your team, build a bracket from spaghetti and masking tape that projects 150 mm out from the table edge and carries a cup hung at its tip. Design rules: you may use up to 20 strands and one metre of tape. Sketch your design and mark where you predict it will fail — and write down the load in kg you predict it will hold.

**Break (52–65 min):** Hang the cup. Add washers steadily until the bracket fails. Weigh the cup and its contents at the moment of failure and record the mass in kg. Note **where** it actually broke and whether that matched your prediction. Post your result to the class scoreboard as an efficiency ratio: failure load in newtons divided by the mass of your bracket in grams.

**Calculate — individually (65–85 min):** On your own results sheet, using your own team's numbers: (1) convert your failure mass to a failure load in newtons; (2) choose a realistic working load for a product a real person would use and justify it in one line; (3) calculate the factor of safety; (4) estimate the cross-sectional area of the member that broke and calculate the stress in it; (5) write one sentence stating whether your FoS is acceptable and what you would change first.

**Scoreboard and exit ticket (85–90 min):** Compare efficiency ratios. The winning team explains their geometry in 30 seconds. Exit ticket: name the structure type of your bracket, and the one force that destroyed it.

</Activity>

<Support title="If a phase is not working">

**FoS calculation strip:** — the same relationship, rearranged three ways.
- $\text{FoS} = \dfrac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}$
- $\text{Working Load} = \dfrac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{FoS}}$
- $\text{Failure Load} = \text{Working Load} \times \text{FoS}$

**Converting mass to force:** multiply kilograms by 10 to get newtons (strictly 9.81, but 10 is accepted and faster). 1.85 kg becomes 18.5 N. Always write the unit — an answer with no unit loses the mark even when the number is right.

**Estimating the area of a spaghetti bundle:** one strand is about 1.8 mm across, so its area is roughly 2.5 mm². Multiply by the number of strands in the member that broke. An estimate that you have stated clearly is worth full marks; a blank is worth none.

**Structure classification — if uncertain:** Ask "does the skin or surface carry the load, or do separate members carry it?" If separate members (rods, beams, struts), it is a frame. If the surface itself carries the load, it is a shell. If it is solid throughout with all the material in compression, it is a mass.

**Bracket keeps failing at the tape, not the spaghetti:** that is a real and reportable result, not a mistake. Note it as a joint failure, and say so in your conclusion — "the structure was limited by its joints" is a strong, mark-worthy engineering sentence.

**Nothing has broken yet and time is running out:** stop loading, record the mass you reached, and write "did not fail at 3.2 kg — test stopped". A lower bound is usable data. Calculate the FoS as a minimum value.

</Support>
