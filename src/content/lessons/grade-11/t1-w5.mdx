You cannot learn materials from a table. You learn them by picking things up — by feeling that aluminium is startlingly light for a metal, that MDF has no grain to split along, that a plastic ruler goes white where you bend it before it snaps. Today the room becomes six testing stations. The properties table gets filled in *after* you have handled everything, from your own evidence.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
Material questions in <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4</ExamLink> and annotations in <ExamLink to="paper1">Paper 1</ExamLink> are marked on one thing: did you name a **specific working property** and link it to a **consequence for the product**? "Strong" earns zero every single time. "High compressive strength, so the shelf will not crush under the load" earns the mark. Handling the material is what makes the specific word available to you under exam pressure.
</Callout>

<Section title="Woods" icon="🌳" id="woods">

### Three families, one decision

| Family | Comes from | Typical examples | Why you would choose it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardwood | Broad-leaved deciduous trees | Oak, beech, ash, mahogany | Hard-wearing surfaces, visible grain, durability |
| Softwood | Coniferous trees | Pine, spruce, Douglas fir | Cheap, light, easy to work — structural framing |
| Manufactured board | Fibres or veneers plus adhesive | MDF, plywood, chipboard | Large stable sheets, no grain, laser-cuts cleanly |

**The trap worth knowing:** hardwood and softwood are *botanical* categories, not hardness measurements. Balsa is a hardwood and you can dent it with a thumbnail. Yew is a softwood and it is harder than several hardwoods. Never justify a choice with "it is a hardwood, so it is hard" — name the actual property.

**For your project:** manufactured board is usually the right answer for laser-cut parts, because it has no grain direction to split along and comes in perfectly flat sheets. Timber strip is usually right for framing members that carry load along their length.

</Section>

<Section title="Metals" icon="🔩" id="metals">

### Ferrous or non-ferrous

The division is simple: **ferrous metals contain iron**, non-ferrous metals do not. That one fact predicts two others — ferrous metals are usually magnetic, and they rust. It is why the magnet at Station 2 sorts the tray in about fifteen seconds.

| Metal | Class | Density (g/cm³) | Notable property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild steel | Ferrous | 7.9 | High tensile strength, cheap, rusts readily |
| Cast iron | Ferrous | 7.2 | Excellent in compression, brittle in tension |
| Stainless steel | Ferrous | 7.9 | Chromium content resists corrosion |
| Aluminium | Non-ferrous | 2.7 | About one third the density of steel; self-protecting oxide layer |
| Copper | Non-ferrous | 8.9 | Outstanding electrical and thermal conductivity, very ductile |
| Brass | Non-ferrous (alloy) | 8.5 | Machines beautifully, corrosion resistant, decorative |

**The density surprise:** most people are genuinely startled by an aluminium block the first time they pick one up, because it looks like steel and weighs a third as much. That is the moment the number 2.7 becomes memorable. It is also the entire reason aircraft are built from it.

<Section title="Composites" icon="🧬" id="composites">

### Matrix and reinforcement

A composite always has two jobs split between two materials. The **matrix** is continuous — it fills the space, holds everything in position, and passes load between the reinforcing elements. The **reinforcement** is discrete — fibres, strands or bars — and it carries the tensile load the matrix could never take alone.

| Composite | Matrix | Reinforcement | What the pairing achieves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reinforced concrete | Cement | Steel bars | Concrete is superb in compression, hopeless in tension; the steel supplies the tension |
| GRP (fibreglass) | Polyester resin | Glass fibres | Strong, light, mouldable into complex curved shells |
| CFRP (carbon fibre) | Epoxy resin | Carbon fibres | Very high strength-to-weight; expensive, hard to repair |
| Plywood | Adhesive | Wood veneers at 90° | Cross-grain layers cancel each other's splitting weakness |

**Why concrete needs steel, in one sentence:** concrete can take roughly ten times more compression than tension, so any concrete beam would crack on its underside — the tension face — under its own load. Steel bars are cast into exactly that face.

</Section>

<Section title="Plastics" icon="♻️" id="plastics">

### The one distinction that matters

| | Thermoplastic | Thermoset |
|---|---|---|
| Heat it | Softens, can be reshaped | Chars and degrades; will not soften |
| Reshape it | Yes, repeatedly | No, never |
| Recycle by melting | Yes | No |
| Examples | PET, PP, ABS, acrylic, PVC | Epoxy, melamine, Bakelite, polyester resin |

The reason thermoplastics can be remelted is that their long molecular chains are only tangled together, not chemically bonded to each other. Heat lets the chains slide. Thermosets form permanent cross-links between the chains during curing — an irreversible chemical change — so there is nothing left to slide.

**The sustainability consequence:** this is why a thermoset is a hard choice to justify in your project. It cannot be recycled by melting, which puts it in direct conflict with the Recycle and Reuse strategies you will audit for homework.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Turning a test result into a mark-worthy justification">

Station 4 gives you an aluminium strip and a mild steel strip of identical size. Here is how the observation becomes an exam answer.

1. **Observe, specifically.** "The aluminium strip is noticeably lighter than the steel strip of the same size, and it bends more easily under hand pressure. Neither is attracted to the magnet — wait, the steel is. The aluminium is not."

2. **Name the property, not the impression.** Lighter for the same volume means lower **density** — about 2.7 g/cm³ against 7.9. Not attracted to the magnet means **non-ferrous**, so it will not rust.

3. **Attach a number or a comparison.** "Aluminium has a density of approximately 2.7 g/cm³, roughly one third that of mild steel."

4. **State the consequence for the product.** "…which means the moving arm of the product can be lifted repeatedly by the user without fatigue."

5. **Assemble the sentence.** *"Aluminium was chosen for the lifting arm because its density of approximately 2.7 g/cm³ — around one third that of mild steel — keeps the arm light enough for the user to raise one-handed. As a non-ferrous metal it also will not rust in the damp garage where the product is stored."*

6. **Check it against the generic-word rule.** No "strong", no "good", no "durable". Two named properties, each with a consequence tied to this user. That is a two-mark answer in both papers.

</WorkedExample>

<Activity title="Six stations, then the table" time="90 min" grouping="Teams of 3–4 rotating; table and justification written individually" materials={["Sample sets: oak, pine, MDF, plywood offcuts", "Sample sets: mild steel, aluminium, copper, brass offcuts", "One strong magnet per station", "Digital kitchen scale and ruler for density work", "Assorted plastic items — PET bottle, acrylic offcut, melamine chip, ABS part", "Broken reinforced concrete or GRP sample if available", "Blank properties table and justification sheet"]}>

**Ground rule:** the properties table stays face down until Phase 7. Everything in it has to come from something you did.

**Blind identification (0–12 min):** Six numbered samples per team, no labels. Handle them, weigh them, scratch them against each other, flex them, tap them and listen. Write your best guess for each and, more importantly, the evidence that led to it. Guesses cost nothing; unevidenced guesses cost the point.

**Station 1 — density by measurement (12–24 min):** Measure and weigh a block of each material and calculate density in g/cm³. Rank your results, then compare against the published figures. Which material surprised you most, and by how much?

**Station 2 — the magnet sort (24–32 min):** Twelve metal samples, one magnet. Sort into ferrous and non-ferrous in under a minute, then write down what else each classification tells you about corrosion behaviour. Find the sample that breaks the rule and explain why austenitic stainless steel is non-magnetic despite containing iron.

**Station 3 — hardness and grain (32–44 min):** Scratch each timber sample with a coin, always with the same pressure, and rank them by resistance. Then try to split each one along its length with gentle thumb pressure at a corner. Note which manufactured boards have no grain to split along, and what that means for a laser-cut part.

**Station 4 — the same shape, different metals (44–54 min):** Identical strips of aluminium and mild steel. Compare mass, bend each by hand, apply the magnet. Then write the two-property justification sentence for a product component using the worked-example structure.

**Station 5 — the plastics sort (54–64 min):** Ten plastic items to classify as thermoplastic or thermoset by reasoning about how each was made and what it does in service. For each thermoplastic, name its recycling route; for each thermoset, explain why melting is not available.

**Station 6 — composites, pulled apart (64–74 min):** Examine the broken samples. Identify the matrix and the reinforcement in each, and find the surface where the reinforcement was doing the work. Explain in one line why removing the reinforcement would change how the sample failed, not just how much load it took.

**Build the table and commit (74–90 min):** Now turn the properties table over and complete it individually, using your measured data wherever you have it. Then write one paragraph naming the material you will use for the main structure of your own project, justified with two named working properties and a consequence for your user. Add it to your C1 brief before you leave.

</Activity>

<Support title="If a phase is not working">

**Justification template:** "[Material] was chosen for [component] because its [named property, with a value or comparison] means [consequence for the product or user]. Its [second property] also [links to function, context or user need]."

**Worked model:** "Oak was chosen for the shelf surface because its hardness means daily use — placing and removing heavy items — will not dent or scratch it. Its attractive grain also meets the user's stated preference for a natural-looking product."

**For non-ferrous metals, a sentence starter:** "As a non-ferrous metal, [material] contains no iron, which means it will not rust — an advantage for a product stored [where the user actually keeps it]."

**Calculating density:** density = mass ÷ volume. Measure the block in centimetres, multiply length × width × height for the volume in cm³, weigh it in grams, then divide. The answer is in g/cm³ and can be compared directly with the published table.

**"I cannot tell these two timbers apart."** Look at the end grain rather than the face. Softwoods show wide, clearly separated growth rings; hardwoods show tighter, denser rings and often visible pores. Weight for the same size is the other reliable clue.

**"My measured density does not match the book."** Expected, and worth saying so. Timber varies with moisture content, offcuts have paint or varnish on them, and a ruler measurement of a rough offcut carries real error. Report your value, state the published one, and give the most likely reason for the gap — that is an evaluation mark, not a failure.

**Five generic words to hunt out of your writing:** strong, durable, good, nice, cheap. Each has a specific replacement: high tensile strength, corrosion resistant, appropriate for the load, meets the user's stated aesthetic preference, low material cost per unit.

</Support>

<Section title="Homework & extension" icon="🏠" id="homework">

**Home task — the 6Rs audit of your own project.** For each of the six Rs — Rethink, Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Repair — write one **specific** action you could take on your own design. Specific means naming a material and a source: "FSC-certified pine from a managed forest" counts, "I will use sustainable materials" does not. Then write two sustainability specification points for your folder, one about material choice and one about end-of-life, each including a test or measure. These two points are assessed inside M2 in Week 8, so they are not optional. Finally, read §3.1 (Types of materials), §3.5 (Composites) and §1.9 (Environment and sustainability).

<Extension title="The honest carbon comparison">
Pick two materials you could realistically use for the same part of your project — say MDF and aluminium. Research the approximate embodied energy of each in MJ per kg, multiply by the mass you would actually use, and compare the totals. Then complicate your own answer: how long would the product have to last for the higher-energy material to be the better environmental choice? Write 120 words. The interesting finding is usually that the lighter-footprint material is not the one you expected.
</Extension>
