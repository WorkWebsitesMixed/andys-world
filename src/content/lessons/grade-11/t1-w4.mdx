Last week you broke things. Today you predict things — and then check whether the maths was telling the truth. A ruler resting on two kitchen scales is a real beam on two real supports, and the numbers on those displays are the reactions you are about to calculate. If your arithmetic is right, you can say what both scales will read *before* the weight goes on.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
Beam reactions are the calculation <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section B</ExamLink> returns to most often, and the mark scheme is generous to anyone who writes the method out: taking moments, substituting, and checking that the reactions sum to the total load are each creditable steps. Joints come up in both the written paper and your project, where the joining method is one of your open design decisions.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="How this session runs">
Each idea comes in the same order: **a quick test, then the notes that explain it.** Everything in a bordered table or a `Formula` box goes into your notebook — those are the definitions and equations you are examined on. Each test says whether it is individual, in pairs, or in a group of 4.
</Callout>

<Section title="Moments — the turning effect" icon="⚖️" id="moments">

### The see-saw you already understand

Everyone knows that a small child can balance a much heavier adult on a see-saw by sitting further out. That intuition *is* the principle of moments, and you can use it as a sanity check on every calculation you do today.

<Callout type="tip" title="Warm-up — the human see-saw · 10 min · groups of 4">
Three problems on the board, argued out in your group before anyone writes a formula.

1. A 20 kg child balances a 60 kg adult sitting 1 m from the pivot. Where must the child sit?
2. The adult moves to 1.5 m. Now where does the child go?
3. Two children, 25 kg and 35 kg, sit on the same side at 2 m and 1 m. One adult of 70 kg balances them — how far out?

You will get these right by instinct. The notes that follow just give a name and a formula to something you can already do.
</Callout>

<Formula
  name="Moment of a force"
  equation="\text{Moment} = \text{Force} \times \text{perpendicular distance}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "\\text{Moment}", meaning: "the turning effect the force has about the pivot — how hard it tries to rotate the object, not how hard it pushes", unit: "N·m or N·mm" },
    { symbol: "\\text{Force}", meaning: "the push or pull applied, converted from mass by multiplying kilograms by about 10", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "\\text{distance}", meaning: "how far the force acts from the pivot, measured at right angles to the line of the force. Keep every distance in the same unit as the answer you want", unit: "m or mm" },
  ]}
  note="Double the distance and you double the turning effect for the same force. That is why the units are a force multiplied by a length."
/>

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Every door handle in the building is on the far edge, never next to the hinge.** That is not styling — it is the longest possible distance from the pivot, so the smallest possible force opens the door. Try pushing a door open two centimetres from its hinge and you will feel exactly what the formula says.

Same principle: a long spanner undoes a tight bolt that a short one cannot, a wheelbarrow lets you lift a load that you could never carry because the wheel is the pivot and your hands are far from it, and a nutcracker, a pair of scissors and a bottle opener are all just two moments about a hinge.
</Callout>

For a structure that is not rotating, the moments must balance:

$$\text{sum of clockwise moments} = \text{sum of anticlockwise moments}$$

### Why we take moments "about A"

Here is the trick that makes beam problems easy. If you take moments about support **A**, then the reaction at A acts *through* the pivot — its distance is zero, so its moment is zero, and it vanishes from the equation entirely. One unknown disappears, and you can solve directly for the other.

That is the whole method. Pick the support you want to eliminate, take moments about it, solve for the other one, then use equilibrium to get the first one back.

</Section>

<Section title="Beam reactions" icon="🌉" id="beam-reactions">

### The method, five steps

1. Draw the beam. Mark supports A and B, and mark the load with its distance from A.
2. Take moments about A: $R_B \times (\text{beam length}) = \text{Load} \times (\text{distance from A})$.
3. Solve for $R_B$.
4. Find $R_A$ using vertical equilibrium: $R_A = \text{Total load} - R_B$.
5. **Check:** $R_A + R_B$ must equal the total load. If it does not, you have made an arithmetic slip — find it before moving on.

### Worked through

A beam 4 m long is supported at A (left) and B (right). A load of 600 N is applied 1 m from A.

<Formula
  name="Moments about A"
  equation="R_B \times 4\ \text{m} = 600\ \text{N} \times 1\ \text{m}"
  substitution="R_B = \frac{600\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}}{4\ \text{m}}"
  result="R_B = 150\ \text{N}"
  terms={[
    { symbol: "R_A", meaning: "the reaction at support A — the upward force that support pushes back with", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "R_B", meaning: "the reaction at support B, the other support", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "W", meaning: "the total downward load on the beam", unit: "N" },
    { symbol: "4\\ \\text{m}", meaning: "the span — the distance between the two supports, which is how far R_B acts from pivot A", unit: "m" },
    { symbol: "1\\ \\text{m}", meaning: "how far the load sits from A, the pivot moments are taken about", unit: "m" },
  ]}
  note="Taking moments about A makes R_A act through the pivot, so its distance is zero and it drops out of the equation entirely — leaving one unknown."
/>

<Formula
  name="Vertical equilibrium"
  equation="R_A = W - R_B"
  substitution="R_A = 600\ \text{N} - 150\ \text{N}"
  result="R_A = 450\ \text{N}"
  note="Check: 450 + 150 = 600 N. The support nearer the load carries the larger share — which matches the see-saw intuition."
/>

<BeamReactionsDiagram />

**The sanity check you should always run:** the support closer to the load always takes the bigger reaction. If your answer says otherwise, you have divided by the wrong distance.

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Two people carrying a heavy box.** If the box slides towards one of you, that person suddenly takes more of the weight — and you can feel the change instantly, without doing any arithmetic. That is a beam on two supports, and you are the supports.

The professional version is everywhere: the two brackets under a shelf, the piers under a bridge, the axles of a lorry (which is why weighbridges measure each axle separately), and the legs of a scaffold tower. Whenever a load sits between two supports, somebody has calculated how the two share it — and if they got it wrong, the nearer one fails first.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Predict the reading, then read it · 22 min · groups of 4">
A ruler bridges two kitchen scales. **Tare both scales with the ruler already resting on them**, or its own weight will show up in every reading.

For each of three load positions — quarter span, midspan, and one-eighth span — do this in order:

1. Calculate both reactions using the five-step method.
2. Convert them to the grams the displays should show.
3. **Write both numbers down.**
4. Only then look at the scales.

Record predicted and actual side by side and work out the percentage difference. Where the two disagree by more than about 10%, the error is in the working, not the equipment — find it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain the disagreement · 8 min · groups of 4">
Every group's prediction and measurement will differ a little. List every reason you can think of — the mass of the ruler itself, friction at the supports, the scale rounding to the nearest gram, the load not sitting exactly where you measured — then rank them by how much you think each one mattered.

Watch the *direction* of the error. If both scales read high, something you left out is pushing down on both. If one reads high and the other low, the moment calculation is wrong. This is precisely the reasoning C7 evaluation asks for in your project.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section title="Structural joints" icon="🔩" id="joints">

A joint is where structures usually fail — as several of you discovered last week when the tape let go before the spaghetti did. Choosing a joint is choosing which force it will have to survive.

| Joint | What it is | Load it handles well | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Halving joint | Both members notched halfway, overlapping flush | Shear at the crossing point | Cross-frames, timber grids |
| Bridle joint | One member slotted, the other tongued — a mechanical interlock | Tension and shear | Corners in timber frames |
| Mortise and tenon | Rectangular peg into a matching hole | Bending and shear | Furniture legs to rails |
| Dowel joint | Round pegs aligning two pieces | Shear | Furniture panels, carcass work |
| KD fitting | Cam lock and bolt, no glue | Tension and compression | Flatpack furniture |

<StructuralJointsDiagram />

**For your project:** the joining method is an open design decision, and mixing methods is normal professional practice — KD fittings plus wood adhesive is a perfectly valid combination. What earns the mark is the *reason*: "dowels, because the load at this corner is shear and the joint will never need to come apart" beats "dowels, because they are strong".

<Callout type="tip" title="Joint puzzle · 9 min · in pairs">
Card strips at your table make up five joints. Assemble each one and identify it from the table above. Then pull, push and twist each until it fails, and decide which of the five forces it resists best.

Sketch three of them in your folder, each with one line on where it would work in your own project — naming the force, not the adjective.
</Callout>

<MoreImages term="timber structural joints halving bridle mortise tenon" />

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**Every flatpack wardrobe you have ever assembled** is held together with KD fittings — the cam lock that you turn a half-turn with the little Allen key. They exist so the thing can be shipped flat and built in a bedroom, and so it can come apart again when you move. They are weaker than glue, and that is a deliberate trade.

Turn over any older wooden chair and you will find mortise and tenon joints where the legs meet the rails, because that joint resists the bending you apply every time you lean back. The chair is a hundred years old and still holding. The flatpack one will not be, and both were correct decisions for their purpose.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Predicting what the scales will read">

This is the calculation you will run in Phase 3, before you are allowed to look at the displays.

1. **Set up.** A 600 mm ruler bridges two kitchen scales. Scale A is at the left end, scale B at the right end, 600 mm apart. Both scales are tared to zero with the ruler already resting on them.

2. **Place the load.** A 1 kg mass is placed 150 mm from scale A. Convert to force: $1 \times 10 = 10\ \text{N}$.

3. **Take moments about A** to eliminate $R_A$.

    <Formula
      name="Moments about A"
      equation="R_B \times 600 = 10 \times 150"
      substitution="R_B = \frac{1{,}500\ \text{N}\cdot\text{mm}}{600\ \text{mm}}"
      result="R_B = 2.5\ \text{N}"
    />

4. **Use equilibrium for the other reaction.**

    <Formula
      name="Vertical equilibrium"
      equation="R_A = W - R_B"
      substitution="R_A = 10\ \text{N} - 2.5\ \text{N}"
      result="R_A = 7.5\ \text{N}"
      note="Check: 7.5 + 2.5 = 10 N. Scale A carries three quarters of the load because the mass sits at one quarter of the span."
    />

5. **Convert back to what the display will actually show.** The scales read grams, not newtons — so divide by 10 and convert: scale A should read about **750 g**, scale B about **250 g**. Write both numbers down, *then* look.

6. **Compare honestly.** Real readings will be a few grams out — friction at the supports, a ruler that is not weightless, scales rounding to the nearest gram. A 3% disagreement confirms the model. A 300% disagreement means you divided by the wrong distance.

</WorkedExample>

This is the whole session, theory included. About a third of it is note-taking and worked method; the scales work doubles as your calculation practice, because every reading is a calculation you did first.

**The rule for the whole session:** the calculation goes on paper *before* anyone looks at a display. A prediction written after the measurement is worth nothing.

**Settle and predict — individual (0–4 min):** The see-saw question is on the board. Write your answer before any discussion.

**Human see-saw warm-up — groups of 4 (4–14 min):** Three balance problems, argued out in your group. No formula yet — you are proving to yourself that you already have the intuition.

**Notes on moments — individual (14–26 min):** Copy the moment formula and the equilibrium statement. Then the reason we take moments about A: the reaction at A acts through the pivot, so its moment is zero and one unknown disappears. That single sentence is what makes every beam problem solvable.

**Notes on the beam method — individual (26–36 min):** Copy the five steps as a method card in your own handwriting, and work the 4 m / 600 N example through with me. Everybody writes step five, the equilibrium check — it is the step that catches errors and it is a mark.

**Predict the scales — groups of 4 (36–58 min):** Three load positions. Calculate, convert to grams, write it down, then look. Record predicted against actual and the percentage difference.

**Explain the disagreement — groups of 4 (58–66 min):** List every reason your numbers and the displays differ, then rank them. Watch whether both readings are high or one is high and one low — that tells you which kind of error you have.

**Joint puzzle — in pairs (76–85 min):** Assemble the five card joints, identify each, then break them and record which force each resists best. Sketch three into your folder.

<Support title="If a phase is not working">

**Beam reaction method card:**
1. Draw the beam, mark supports A and B, mark the load with its position.
2. Take moments about A: (RB × beam length) = (Load × distance from A).
3. Solve for RB.
4. Find RA = Total load − RB.
5. Check: RA + RB must equal the total load.

**"Which distance do I multiply by?"** The load is multiplied by its distance *from the pivot you chose*. The reaction you are solving for is multiplied by the *full span*. If you take moments about A, every distance is measured from A.

**"My two reactions do not add up to the load."** That is the check doing its job. Ninety per cent of the time the error is using the distance from the wrong end for the load. Re-measure from A and redo step 2.

**"The scales disagree with my answer."** First check the obvious: did you tare the scales with the ruler already on them? An untared ruler adds its own weight to both readings. Then check whether the load is really where you think it is — measure to the centre of the mass, not its edge.

**Reading the display in grams when the maths is in newtons:** work the whole calculation in newtons, then divide the answer by 10 to get kilograms and multiply by 1,000 for grams. Or simply do the whole calculation in grams — because the reactions are a ratio of the load, the units carry through unchanged.

**Choosing a joint when they all look plausible:** ask two questions in order. Does this joint ever need to come apart again? If yes, KD fitting. If no, which force dominates here — shear across the joint, or bending trying to fold it? Shear points to a dowel or halving joint; bending points to a mortise and tenon.

</Support>
