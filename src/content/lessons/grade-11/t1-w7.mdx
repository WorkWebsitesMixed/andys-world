<Activity plan title="M2 quiz, then specification and Spec Court" time="90 min" grouping="Individual for M2 and writing; pairs and groups of 4 for the games" materials={["Exam paper (M2)", "Black pen for M2 (no pencil)", "Pencil and red pen", "Bounty hunt answer cards", "Mark scheme extracts", "Your C1 design brief from Week 1", "Your C1 pages and C2 research (parts A to E of the C2 guide)", "C2 guide", "Your 6Rs audit from Week 5", "Ruler for measuring an existing product"]}>

**M2 quiz — individual, exam conditions (0–20 min):** Exam conditions. No notes, no discussion, no assistance. This one is silent and it is yours.

**Be the examiner — in pairs (20–35 min):** Swap M2 papers. Using the real mark scheme, mark your partner's paper as an examiner would, writing the mark and the reason beside each answer. For every question below full marks, circle the exact word or phrase that lost it. Hand the paper back and read what was written about yours.

**Bounty hunt — groups of 4 (35–42 min):** Hunt the generic words on the six answer cards, which I wrote to contain the usual traps. A specific replacement that is wrong scores minus two. The five biggest offenders (strong, durable, good, better, appropriate) are worth double. Cards 3 and 6 also hide an error in the working, worth 2 points each.

**Specification writing — individual (42–66 min):** Write at least six specification points using the three-part template: criterion, measure, justification. Draw on your conclusions page (what you will take forward) and on a measured number from a real product wherever you can. Cover load, dimensions, aesthetics, material and sustainability, safety, and the circuit. For each point, underline the measure and circle the justification. If you cannot do both, the point is not finished.

**Spec Court — in pairs (66–80 min):** Adversarial on purpose. Hand your specification to your partner, who designs in words the worst possible product that still passes every point. Every loophole they find is a point to them and a tightening for you. Swap and prosecute theirs.

**Tighten and submit — individual (80–90 min):** Fix every loophole found against you. Then run the submission checklist: named user, three or more function points, six or more specification points, a measure on each, a justification on each. Fix anything missing now, because the folder is collected as you leave.
</Activity>

<Section title="The M2 quiz format" icon="🎯" id="quiz-format">

### What Section A looks like

Paper 4 Section A consists of approximately 10–15 short-answer questions, each worth 1–4 marks. Questions test:
- Identification (name a component, identify a structure type, label a force)
- Description (describe what an NTC thermistor does)
- Explanation (explain why concrete is reinforced)
- Calculation (find beam reaction, FoS, resistor value)

Total marks for Section A: approximately 40. Recommended time: 40–45 minutes in the real Paper 4.

### Today's M2 quiz

Same format, shorter version: 8–10 questions covering T1 W1–W6 content. 20 minutes.

**Strategy:** Read each question fully before answering. If a question has multiple parts, answer all parts. Never leave a question blank — a blank earns zero; a partial answer may earn a mark.

</Section>

<Section title="The no-generic-words rule" icon="✏️" id="generic-words">

### Why generic words earn zero

Paper 4 mark schemes use a principle called **positive marking**: marks are awarded for specific correct statements, not for generally sensible-sounding answers. "Steel is strong" matches no mark point. "Mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa" matches the mark point for "high tensile strength with a specific value or comparison."

<Notebook title="The rewrite process">

For every generic word in an answer, ask:
1. **What specific property does 'strong' / 'durable' / 'good' refer to?** (tensile strength? hardness? corrosion resistance?)
2. **What is the measured value or comparison?** (400 MPa? three times higher than aluminium? lasts 20 years without treatment?)
3. **What is the consequence for the product?** (means the bracket resists bending under a 5 kg load without permanent deformation)

</Notebook>

### The five generic words that lose the most marks

| Generic word | Specific replacement |
|---|---|
| Strong | "High tensile strength (approx. 400 MPa for mild steel)" |
| Durable | "High corrosion resistance — anodised aluminium forms a passive oxide layer" or "High hardness — resists surface scratching from daily use" |
| Good | Identify what specific property is good (hardness? cost? availability?) and name it |
| Light | "Low density (2.7 g/cm³ for aluminium — approximately one-third the density of steel)" |
| Rigid | "High Young's modulus (stiffness) — resists elastic deformation under load" |

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Rewriting vague answers into mark-worthy ones">

**Q: Explain why mild steel is a suitable material for a structural shelf bracket.**

Vague: "Mild steel is strong and durable, which makes it good for a shelf bracket."
Rewritten: "Mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa, which means the bracket's arms resist bending when loaded with 5 kg without permanent deformation. It can also be powder-coated to prevent corrosion in the indoor environment where the shelf is mounted."

---

**Q: Give one reason why a tilt switch is used in this circuit.**

Vague: "The tilt switch is appropriate for this product."
Rewritten: "The tilt switch closes when the product is lifted, activating the LED automatically — without requiring the user to press a separate button."

---

**The same rule applied to a specification point.**

Vague: "The shelf must be strong enough."
Rewritten: "The product must support a static vertical load of at least 5 kg for 24 hours without permanent deformation, as tested by applying a 5 kg deadweight, because the user stores textbooks with a measured total mass of 4.3 kg."
</WorkedExample>

<Section title="Writing a measurable, justified specification (C2)" icon="📋" id="specification">

### Where your evidence comes from

You arrive with your C2 research and its conclusions page: your interviews, your measurements of a real product and of the real place, and what each one told you. The strongest justification you can have is a number you measured yourself on a real product or in your user's real space. Secondary research (manufacturer specifications, safety standards) fills the gaps. For example, an LED circuit under 50 V DC counts as Safe Extra-Low Voltage, which is a ready-made safety criterion.

An ACCESS FM pass over one existing product, measured with a ruler, is the quickest way to turn "I looked at a shelf" into usable numbers: Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Safety, Size, Function, Materials.

### The three-part specification point

<Notebook title="The three-part specification point">

1. **Criterion:** what the product must do or be.
2. **Measure:** how the criterion will be tested (specific, not vague).
3. **Justification:** why this criterion is required (linked to research).

**Template:** "The product must [criterion] as tested by [test method], because [research finding]."

</Notebook>

| Poor (unacceptable) | Strong (mark-worthy) |
|---|---|
| "It must look good." | "The product must have a surface finish with no visible machining marks, as tested by visual inspection at 300 mm, because the user stated they would place the product on a visible desk." |
| "It must be safe." | "All exposed edges must be rounded or chamfered to a radius of at least 1 mm, as tested by running a finger along all edges, because the product will be used in daily contact and sharp edges pose a laceration risk." |
| "It must fit the desk." | "The product width must not exceed 590 mm, as measured with a steel rule, because the user's available desk depth is 600 mm and the product must not overhang the desk edge." |

<Notebook title="Six areas to cover">

Your six specification points should span: load/structural performance, dimensions (size), aesthetics/finish, materials (sustainability), safety, and electronics (circuit function). Two of them must be the sustainability points from your Week 5 6Rs audit.

</Notebook>

<Callout type="info" title="Where you'll see this">
**"IP67" on the back of a phone is a specification point.** It does not say "water resistant" — it says the device survives immersion in one metre of water for thirty minutes, which is a criterion somebody can actually test and either pass or fail.

Every product you own was signed off against a list like the one you are writing: a kettle against BS EN 60335, a bike helmet against a specified impact test, a toy against a small-parts gauge. When a product is recalled, it is almost always because it failed a specific measurable clause — never because it was "not good enough".
</Callout>

</Section>

<Support title="Rewrite scaffolds and specification troubleshooting">

**Three-step rewrite frame:**
1. Name the specific property: "[material] has high/low [property name]..."
2. Add a value or comparison: "...approximately [value with units] / compared to [alternative]..."
3. State the consequence: "...which means [consequence for the product's function or performance]."

**Units conversion card:**
- mA to A: divide by 1,000 (20 mA = 0.020 A)
- kΩ to Ω: multiply by 1,000 (4.7 kΩ = 4,700 Ω)
- N/mm² and MPa are the same unit (1 N/mm² = 1 MPa)
- g/cm³ to kg/m³: multiply by 1,000 (2.7 g/cm³ = 2,700 kg/m³)

**"I cannot think of a test for this point."** If you cannot describe a test, the criterion is not yet measurable. Add a number, a dimension, or a condition. "It must look professional" becomes "It must have a consistent painted finish with no brush marks visible at 500 mm, as assessed by visual inspection."

**"I do not have enough research for justifications."** Use the measurements from an existing product as secondary research: "Measurement of a comparable product found on the market showed a width of 500 mm — this defines the upper size boundary for this product."

**"My specification is just restating the brief."** The specification refines the brief with numbers. If the brief says "it must hold textbooks," the spec says "it must support 5 kg vertically at the shelf midspan with less than 2 mm deflection."

**How to run Spec Court so it stays useful:** the attacker is not allowed to argue that a point is badly written — only to describe a genuinely terrible product that still satisfies it as written. That keeps the exercise about testability rather than style, and stops it becoming personal. Model it first with a deliberately weak point of your own: "The product must be safe to use", passed by a product that is safe because it does nothing.

**The most common loopholes** are unquantified verbs (hold, support, fit, illuminate), missing conditions (for how long? under what load? measured from where?), and criteria that describe the designer's intention rather than an observable outcome.

</Support>

<Section title="Homework" icon="🏠" id="homework">

**Home task:** Review your M2 script. For every mark you missed, write a corrected full-mark answer, and rewrite every answer that used a generic word (specific property, then a value or comparison, then the consequence). Then write your three personal rules for avoiding generic vocabulary, in your own words, and tape them inside your folder cover.

If any specification points were incomplete when the folder was collected, finish them for your own records. Read §1.8 (Product design) in the Collins textbook and note two additional criteria types you had not considered.
