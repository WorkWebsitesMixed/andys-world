This is the session that defines the next eighteen months of your D&T life. You will not be picking a product today — you will be picking a **person**. Three speed interviews, a wall of real problems, and a thirty-second pitch you have to survive. By the time you leave you will know who you are building for and what it must do, and everything from here to the final submission in Term 3 flows from that.

<Section title="The Component 2 brief" icon="📋" id="brief">

### Five fixed constraints — everything else is your decision

Every student in this class is making the same type of product, but no two products will look the same. The five **fixed constraints** that apply to everyone:

1. **Carry a defined load** — a weight you state, measure and test.
2. Include a working **low-voltage, switch-operated circuit** (a light, indicator or similar).
3. Be made from the **workshop material palette**, using the school makerspace.
4. **Fit within a 400 × 400 mm** footprint, with at least one dimension **≥ 150 mm**.
5. Be a **real, finished product — not a model** — made to a standard your user would be proud to own.

Everything else is your design decision: who your user is and their context, what problem your product solves, what your product is, its dimensions, structure, joints, finish, and how the circuit fits it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Speed interviews · 24 min · in pairs, rotating">
Three rounds of eight minutes, a different partner each round, four minutes each way. You may not repeat a partner.

Ask: what do you store or organise that has no good home? Where would this product live? Do you use that space at night — would a light help? What exactly needs storing, and how heavy is it?

Write only what they actually said, in their words. Three interviews means three candidate users: you are shopping, not settling.
</Callout>

</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Three radically different briefs — same constraints, different users">

**User A — Maya, 16, musician:** Guitar cables, picks, capo, and phone charger live in a pile on her desk. She needs an organised storage system at desk height with a built-in light for practising at night.

**Brief A:** Design a wall-mounted desk organiser for a teenage guitarist that holds at least four guitar accessories, supports a phone-charging cable without tangling, and includes a switch-operated LED to illuminate the workspace without glare.

---

**User B — Carlos, 15, cyclist:** Cycling helmet, gloves, lights, and repair kit pile up by the front door. He needs a hook-and-shelf unit that keeps gear accessible.

**Brief B:** Design a freestanding entry-hall organiser for a teenage cyclist that stores a helmet (mass approximately 350 g), gloves, and bike tools, with a switch-operated indicator LED to confirm the space is occupied.

---

All three constraints are met. The products look nothing alike.
</WorkedExample>

<Activity title="Speed interviews, profile and brief" time="90 min" grouping="Rotating pairs, then individual writing" materials={["A4 paper", "Pen", "Small card for term target", "Sticky notes", "Timer visible to the room"]}>

**Speed interviews, three rounds — in pairs, rotating (0–24 min):** Three rounds of eight minutes, a different partner each round, and you must not repeat a partner. Four minutes each way. Ask the prompts on the board and write only what they actually said, in their words. Three interviews means three candidate users — you are shopping, not settling.

**The problem wall — individual, then whole class (24–34 min):** Write the single best problem you heard on a sticky note — one sentence, naming a real person and a real frustration — and put it on the wall. Read everyone else's. Steal shamelessly: if someone else's problem is more interesting than yours, that is useful information about your own.

**Pick your person and profile them — individual (34–58 min):** Choose the user you will actually design for. On one A4 sheet: name or pseudonym, age, context (where the product lives), two or three problems they face, and one direct quote. The quote is not decoration — it is the evidence that this user is real, and C1 credits it.

**Write the brief — individual, then swap in pairs (58–78 min):** One sentence saying who the user is and what the product is. Then three or more function points, each starting "The product must…" and each testable. Then one user-specific constraint beyond the fixed ones. Swap with a neighbour and play **spot the wish**: circle every point your partner could not actually test. Hand it back and fix yours.

**Thirty-second pitch — individual, to the room (78–86 min):** Stand and pitch your user and brief to the room in thirty seconds. The timer is strict and it is visible. If you cannot say who it is for and what it must do in thirty seconds, the brief is not focused yet — and that is the point of the exercise, not a punishment.

**Term target card — individual (86–90 min):** One personal learning goal for Term 1, specific enough to check: "I will annotate every sketch with material, join and function before calling it done."
</Activity>

<Support title="Sentence starters for the brief">

**Opening:** "Design a [product type] for [user] who [context]."

**Function points:** "The product must [verb] [specific thing] [measurable if possible]."
- "The product must hold five textbooks (total mass approximately 4 kg) upright and accessible."
- "The product must include a switch-operated LED that illuminates the workspace without glare."

**Mistake to avoid:** Do not describe the form. "Made of wood with natural finish" is a design decision, not a function point. The brief states what it must *do*, not what it must *look like*.
</Support>
