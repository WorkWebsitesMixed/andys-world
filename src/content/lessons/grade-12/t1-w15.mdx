<Section title="Building today's topic" icon="💬" id="topic">

### Why do computers use binary?

Not because it's efficient for humans — it obviously isn't. It's because electronic circuits have exactly **two** reliable states: on and off. Binary's two digits map directly onto that physical reality. Paper 1 rarely asks you to do binary arithmetic — the concept (why binary, not how to convert) is what gets tested.

### The storage unit ladder

bit → byte → KB → MB → GB → TB, each one roughly a thousand times the last. "How many bytes in a kilobyte?" has two acceptable answers — 1024 (the technically correct power-of-two value) or 1000 (the SI approximation) — examiners accept either, so don't panic about which one a question implies unless it tells you.

### File size calculation — walk through every step

*"An image is 800 × 600 pixels, and each pixel uses 24 bits of colour. What is the file size?"*

$$
800 \times 600 \times 24 = 11{,}520{,}000 \text{ bits}
$$
$$
11{,}520{,}000 \div 8 = 1{,}440{,}000 \text{ bytes} \approx 1.37 \text{ MB}
$$

Three steps, every time: total pixels × bits per pixel = total bits. Divide by 8 to get bytes. Convert up to a sensible unit. Skipping the divide-by-8 step is the single most common way this question loses marks.

### Data types — why does it matter, not just what they're called?

Text/string, integer, real/float, Boolean, date — naming them is the easy half. The exam wants to know *why the choice matters*: store a phone number as an integer, and its leading zero silently disappears (`0412...` becomes `412...`), because integers don't keep leading zeros. Store it as text instead, and the exact digits are preserved.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Weak answer → improved answer (binary)">
**Question:** "Explain why a computer's data is ultimately represented in binary."

Weak: *"Because computers only understand 1s and 0s."*

Improved: *"A computer's electronic circuits have two stable states — on and off. Binary uses exactly two digits, 0 and 1, so each digit can be represented directly by one of those two states, making binary the natural number system for electronic hardware."*

The improvement earns marks by explaining **the physical reason** (two circuit states), not just restating that computers "understand" binary as if it were a given.
</WorkedExample>

<Activity title="Short-answer practice" time="17 min" grouping="Individual" materials={["Paper or notebook", "Calculator"]}>
Show full working for the calculation — the final answer alone will not earn full marks.

1. An image is 1000 × 800 pixels, using 8 bits of colour per pixel. Calculate the file size in bytes, then convert to a sensible unit.
2. A form asks for a UK postcode (e.g. "SW1A 1AA"). State the most appropriate data type, and explain why an integer would be unsuitable.
3. Explain, in one sentence, why the Boolean data type is suitable for storing an answer to "Is this customer over 18?"
</Activity>

<Support title="Mark scheme — check your answers">
1. $1000 \times 800 \times 8 = 6{,}400{,}000$ bits. $6{,}400{,}000 \div 8 = 800{,}000$ bytes ≈ **0.76 MB**.
2. **Text/string** — a postcode contains letters as well as digits, and treating it as a number would be meaningless (you can't do arithmetic on a postcode) and would also risk losing formatting such as the space in the middle.
3. A Boolean stores exactly one of two values, true or false — which matches a yes/no question like "is this customer over 18?" precisely, with no wasted storage or ambiguity.
</Support>
