<Section title="Building today's topic" icon="💬" id="topic">

### Input devices — beyond keyboard and mouse

Name an input device that isn't a keyboard or a mouse, and for each one ask what data it captures and in what form:

- **Scanner** — a physical document or image → a digital image file.
- **Microphone** — sound → digital audio.
- **Barcode reader** — a printed barcode → a product code.
- **Sensor** (temperature, light, pressure) — a physical condition → a numeric reading, sampled continuously.

The exam wants the *specific* capture, not just "it takes in data." A microphone doesn't "input sound" for full marks — it "converts sound waves into a digital audio signal the computer can store and process."

### Output devices — what's actually different about them

A monitor and a printer both show you the result of processing, so what's the real distinction? **Permanence.** A monitor gives soft copy — temporary, on-screen, gone when you turn it off. A printer gives hard copy — permanent, physical, exists independently of the computer. That single word, *permanent* vs *temporary*, is what the mark scheme is checking for.

### Storage: what happens to data in RAM when the power goes off?

Nothing survives — that's the volatile/non-volatile distinction, and it's the single most-tested idea in this topic. Three physical storage types, each with a trade-off:

| Type | Examples | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnetic | Hard disk drive (HDD) | High capacity, low cost per GB | Moving parts — fragile, slower |
| Optical | DVD, Blu-ray | Portable, cheap to produce | Slower; scratch damage |
| Solid-state | SSD, USB drive | Fast, no moving parts, robust | More expensive per GB |

### RAM vs ROM — the question that catches almost everyone

"Why can't the computer boot from RAM alone?" Work it through: RAM is volatile, so the instant power is removed, it's empty. If the boot instructions lived only in RAM, there would be nothing there to read when the computer is switched back on. ROM is non-volatile — it keeps the boot instructions safe with no power at all, which is exactly why it's ROM's job and not RAM's.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Weak answer → improved answer (input devices)">
**Question:** "A shop uses a device to read barcodes on products at the checkout. Describe how this device works."

Weak: *"A scanner is used to put pictures into a computer."*

Improved: *"A barcode reader captures the pattern of black and white lines printed on a product's barcode, and converts it into a digital product code that the computer can look up in a database to find the price and description."*

The improvement earns marks by naming the **specific device** for the scenario (not a generic scanner), stating **exactly what it captures** (the barcode pattern), and explaining **what happens to the data** (converted to a code, used to look something up) — not just that data "goes into the computer."
</WorkedExample>

<Support title="Mark scheme — check your answers">
1. The laptop lost the document because it was held in **RAM**, which is **volatile** — its contents are lost the instant power is removed. Only work that had already been saved to non-volatile storage (the hard disk or SSD) survives a power cut.
2. **Magnetic hard disk** — high capacity at low cost per GB, which suits a large archive where speed matters less than storing a lot of data affordably. (A solid-state answer with "fast access, no moving parts" is also creditable if the scenario justifies needing speed.)
3. Solid-state storage has **no moving parts**, so it is more robust against physical shock and generally faster, because there is no read head that needs to physically move to locate data — unlike a magnetic disk, where the head must seek to the correct track before it can read.
</Support>
