The regular class's checklist gets a working drawing to "complete" — three views, dimensions, title block, third-angle symbol. This session pushes two things the regular class only offers as an optional extension: assembly and sectional views, taught properly rather than left for anyone with spare time. It also opens the door to a vocabulary real engineering drawings use that goes beyond a single dimension number.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="project">Component 2 Criterion 5</ExamLink> rewards a working drawing set someone unfamiliar with your project could actually build from — and a drawing missing an internal feature (a cable channel, an assembly relationship) fails that test even if every external dimension is present and correct.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Assembly drawing", definition: "A drawing showing how separate components fit together, usually with each part numbered and a parts list alongside — communicates assembly order and the relationship between parts, not just one part's own dimensions." },
  { term: "Exploded view", definition: "A drawing showing all the components of an assembly pulled apart along their assembly axis, but still aligned, so the order and direction of assembly is visible at a glance — commonly used in flat-pack furniture instructions for exactly this reason." },
  { term: "Sectional view", definition: "A drawing showing the object as if cut through a chosen plane, revealing internal geometry (a cable channel, an internal cavity) that would otherwise need several hidden-detail dashed lines to describe — often clearer than hidden detail for genuinely complex internal features." },
  { term: "GD&T (Geometric Dimensioning & Tolerancing)", definition: "A formal symbolic system for specifying not just a dimension's size, but its exact geometric requirements — flatness, perpendicularity, position relative to a reference — using standardised symbols in a feature control frame, rather than a plain dimension number and a general tolerance." },
]} />

<Section title="Assembly, exploded, and sectional views — taught, not left optional" icon="🧩" id="views">

The regular class's Week 8 extension mentions a section view as optional extra rigour. Here's the full set of three views a complete working-drawing package uses, and when each earns its place:

| View type | Shows | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Assembly | How separate components fit together, numbered with a parts list | Your product has more than one major sub-assembly (a base + a bracket + a circuit panel) |
| Exploded | Components pulled apart along the assembly axis, still aligned | The assembly ORDER isn't obvious from the assembled view alone |
| Sectional | The object cut through a chosen plane, revealing internal geometry | An internal feature (cable channel, cavity) would otherwise need several hidden-detail lines to describe clearly |

**Choosing a section plane:** pick the plane that passes through the internal feature you need to show, in a direction that keeps the section simple to read — usually straight through the centre of the feature, not at an angle. Hatch the cut surfaces at 45° to distinguish "material that was cut through" from "material you're looking at the surface of."

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Adding an assembly, exploded, and sectional view to the gear organiser drawing set">

**Assembly view:** base panel, two side uprights, top rail, and the switch bracket, numbered 1–4, with a small parts list in the corner naming each and its material.

**Exploded view:** the same four parts pulled apart vertically along the assembly axis, still aligned above/below where they connect — makes clear the side uprights attach to the base first, then the top rail connects the uprights, then the switch bracket clips on last.

**Sectional view:** a cutting plane straight through the base panel where the internal cable channel runs from the switch bracket to the battery compartment — revealing the channel's depth and width, which no combination of hidden-detail dashed lines on the exterior views could show as clearly. Cut surfaces hatched at 45°.

**Why all three matter for C5:** the assembly and exploded views prove the maker can understand assembly *order*, not just individual part dimensions; the sectional view proves an internal feature invisible from outside is still fully specified — together, exactly what "someone could build this unaided" requires.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: GD&T — dimensioning beyond a single number">

A plain dimension ("50 mm ±1 mm") only specifies size. Real engineering drawings often need to specify *geometric* requirements too — is a face actually flat, not just the right size? Is a hole positioned exactly relative to an edge, not just somewhere within a size tolerance? **GD&T** answers this with standardised symbols in a **feature control frame** — a small rectangular box attached to the dimension, containing a symbol (flatness, perpendicularity, position, and many others), a tolerance value, and often a reference to a **datum** (a fixed reference surface everything else is measured from).

| Symbol meaning | What it controls | Why a plain dimension can't |
|---|---|---|
| Flatness | How flat a surface actually is, regardless of its size | A face can be the right length and width and still be warped |
| Perpendicularity | Whether two faces are truly at 90°, not just close | Two faces can each be individually "the right size" and still not meet squarely |
| Position | Whether a hole is exactly where it should be relative to a datum, not just "somewhere in range" | A hole's diameter can be perfect while its location drifts |

You won't be expected to apply full GD&T at IGCSE, but recognising that "dimensioned" and "geometrically correct" are different questions — and that a symbolic system exists specifically for the second one — is exactly the kind of engineering-standards literacy that separates a working knowledge of technical drawing from a professional one.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Adding assembly/exploded/section views, GD&T taste, TA-prep" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD + drawing colleague circulating" materials={["Your working drawing from the regular Week 8/9 class", "A3 paper", "Ruler, technical pen, set square"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Recap third-angle projection, plan and elevations, scale, and BS dimensioning from the regular class.

**10–25 min — Complete BS drawing.** Add an assembly view (numbered parts, parts list) to your working drawing set. If your product has an internal feature not visible from outside, add a sectional view through it.

**25–35 min — GD&T taste.** Read the extension box. Identify one feature on your own product where flatness or perpendicularity — not just a size dimension — genuinely matters for the product to work correctly.

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: produce a dimensioned assembly drawing plus one sectional view for your product.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: 'could someone build it from this alone?'">

**Common error to watch for:** a missing or ambiguous dimension — a feature that's drawn but never actually measured on the page, leaving a maker to guess.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Could someone build it from this alone?"* — the direct test for completeness; if the honest answer involves "well, they'd have to guess that bit," the drawing isn't finished yet.

</Support>
