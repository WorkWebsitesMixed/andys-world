Today's regular class closes C4 (worth 15 marks) and submits it for M2. This session applies the exact same standard you built for specifications in Term 1's SK6 — **traceability** — to something different: not a spec point this time, but every design change in your development story. It also extends the T1 SK3 embodied-energy calculation to a genuinely new question: what happens to your material at end of life, and can your design make that easier?

<Section title="Traceability, applied to development" icon="🔗" id="traceable-dev">

T1 SK6 established the traceability chain for specification points: research finding → spec point → test method. C4 needs the same discipline applied to a different chain: **test result → design change → re-test confirmation.**

| Level | Example | The problem |
|---|---|---|
| Resolved, not traceable | "I widened the base because it looked more stable." | "Looked more stable" isn't evidence — where's the test that showed the original was unstable? |
| Traceable | "I widened the base from 150 mm to 210 mm because the Week 5 asymmetric-load test showed tipping at 2.7 kg, below the 4 kg spec. Re-tested after widening: no tipping at 3.5 kg asymmetric." | The change traces to an exact test result, and the re-test confirms the fix worked. |

Go through your own C4 pages and check every design change against this: can you name the exact test result that drove it? If the answer is "I just thought it needed it," that change isn't distinction-level evidence yet — even if the change itself was the right call.

</Section>

<Section title="Design for disassembly: the 6R the regular class covers, taken further" icon="♻️" id="disassembly">

The regular class's sustainability table already lists "knock-down fittings allow disassembly and recyclable components at end of life" as one decision among several. **Design for disassembly** makes that its own deliberate design question, not just one line in a table: at end of life, can the different materials in your product actually be separated, or are they permanently bonded into one mixed-material lump that can only go to landfill?

Revisit T1 SK1's joint-selection table with this lens: a corner block glued in place resists a moment well (SK1's structural reasoning), but it's now permanently bonded — good for strength, bad for disassembly. A KD fitting resists the same moment mechanically, without glue, and can be unscrewed at end of life. This is a genuine trade-off (T1 SK7's three-part annotation model applies directly): strength and ease-of-assembly vs disassembly and recyclability, and a distinction-level sustainability note names the trade-off explicitly rather than treating KD fittings as a free upgrade with no downside.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Extending T1 SK3's embodied energy calculation to end-of-life">

T1 SK3 calculated embodied energy (mass × specific embodied energy) for making a component. End-of-life adds a second question: how much of that embodied energy can be *recovered* through recycling, versus lost to landfill?

**Steel bracket arm (0.14 kg, ≈3.5 MJ embodied energy from T1 SK3):** steel is one of the most-recycled materials in the world, and recycling it recovers a large share of that embodied energy — a design that allows the steel to be separated out (not glued to other materials) means most of that 3.5 MJ isn't wasted at end of life.

**Design-for-disassembly note:** "The steel bracket arm is joined with a KD (knock-down) fitting rather than adhesive, specifically so it can be separated from the timber backboard at end of life and sent to metal recycling rather than landfill as a mixed-material assembly. This trades a small amount of joint rigidity (KD fittings resist a moment slightly less stiffly than a glued corner block, per SK1's joint table) for recovering most of the material's embodied energy at end of life."

This is what a genuinely distinction-level sustainability paragraph looks like: it names a specific number from earlier work (embodied energy), a specific design decision (KD fitting), and the specific trade-off it costs (slightly less joint rigidity) — not just "recyclable materials were used."

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: life-cycle assessment and the circular economy at product scale">

A full **life-cycle assessment** (T1 SK3's taste-beyond topic) traces environmental impact across a product's entire life — extraction, manufacture, use, and disposal — not just the manufacturing stage the embodied-energy calculation covers. The **circular economy** is the design philosophy this points toward: instead of a one-way line from raw material to landfill, materials circulate — a disassembled product's steel becomes a new product's steel, its timber becomes another product's timber, indefinitely, rather than being extracted once and discarded once. Every design-for-disassembly decision you make this session is a small, concrete instance of designing for a circular economy rather than a linear one.

</Extension>

<Support title="TA prep: 'what evidence drove this change?'">

**Common error to watch for:** a peer's development page shows a nice final design but no evidence of what problem it fixed or why the change was made.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"What evidence drove this change?"* — if they can't point to a specific test result, help them either find it (maybe it exists but wasn't written down) or generate it now with a quick re-test.
