<Section title="The swap test: a sharper check than the category list" icon="🔄" id="swap-test">

The regular class's structural categories (shell / frame / freestanding / wall-mounted / suspended) are a good starting point, but three ideas can use three different category *labels* and still fail to be genuinely different underneath. The **swap test** catches this:

> Could you take one specific feature from Idea 1 — its material, its joint, its exact form — and drop it into Idea 2 without changing what Idea 2 fundamentally *is*? If yes, the two ideas share their real concept and only differ in surface detail.

A wall-mounted pegboard frame and a wall-mounted slatted frame both pass the "wall-mounted" category, but if you could swap "pegboard" for "slats" without changing anything else about how either idea works structurally, they fail the swap test — they're the same underlying idea. A wall-mounted cantilever bracket and a freestanding A-frame pass the swap test easily: swapping a feature between them doesn't even make sense, because their whole structural logic is different.

</Section>

<Section title="Fast-sketch technique for timed conditions" icon="✏️" id="fast-sketch">

Three genuinely different ideas, each properly annotated, in roughly 15–18 minutes total means under 6 minutes per idea. That's not enough time for careful, measured drawing — it requires a specific, practised technique:

1. **Construction lines first, light and fast** — a rough crate or axis sketch, barely pressing the pencil, gets the proportions roughly right before any detail exists.
2. **Resolve form before detail** — decide the overall shape completely before adding a single annotation or internal line. Detail on an unresolved form gets redrawn, wasting the time you don't have.
3. **Never erase — draw over** — stopping to erase costs more time than a confidently redrawn line on top of a wrong one. Under time pressure, correction by addition beats correction by removal.
4. **Consistent scale by eye, not by measuring** — a ruler and careful measurement is a Week 8 CAD skill, not a Paper 1 part (c) skill. Keep proportions roughly consistent across all three ideas by eye; exact dimensions belong in the annotations, not in measured construction.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Three ideas, swap-tested, for a plant-collector display stand">

**Idea 1 — Tiered ladder frame:** stepped horizontal shelves on a vertical ladder frame. **Idea 2 — Cantilevered wall bracket:** single swept arm from a wall fixing. **Idea 3 — Rotating column:** central pillar with a lazy-susan tray.

**Swap test, applied:** could the ladder frame's "stepped shelves" feature be dropped into the cantilever bracket idea? No — the cantilever bracket has one arm, not multiple shelves; the feature doesn't transplant without redesigning the whole idea. Could the rotating tray be dropped into the ladder frame? No — a ladder frame has no rotation axis to attach it to. All three ideas pass the swap test against each other: genuinely different structural concepts, not the same idea with swapped details.

**Fast-sketch application:** each idea gets a light construction crate first (roughly 60–90 seconds), then form resolved with confident single lines (roughly 2 minutes), then three technical annotations added last (roughly 1–2 minutes) — under 5 minutes per idea, leaving buffer time for the strongest idea to get an extra annotation.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: rapid concept generation under a deadline">

Professional design teams pitching to a client on a fixed deadline face exactly this problem at a larger scale: produce several genuinely different concepts, fast, with no time to fully resolve any of them. The discipline is the same — construction-first, form before detail, keep moving rather than perfecting one idea — and the same insight about constraints applies: a completely open brief with unlimited time often produces *fewer* genuinely different concepts than a tight deadline, because unlimited time invites endless refinement of the first idea rather than the discipline of starting fresh. The 18-minute exam constraint is, in a real sense, training the exact skill a professional pitch deadline demands.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Timed three-idea practice, swap-tested" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["A3 paper", "Pencil only — no ruler", "A stopwatch or phone timer"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Recap what part (c) asks, the common trap of three variations of one idea, and time discipline (roughly 18 minutes / 5–6 min per idea).

**10–28 min — Top-band three-ideas practice.** Given a past-paper context, produce three ideas under an 18-minute timer using fast-sketch technique. Swap-test all three pairs against each other before annotating.

**28–36 min — Professional ideation, taste.** Read the extension box. Note one similarity between the exam's time constraint and a real design-pitch deadline.

**36–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: produce three genuinely different part (c) responses under timed conditions, mark them to the scheme, and annotate exactly what earned and lost marks.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: the quality-anchor role">

**This week you're not just tutoring — you're the visible standard.** Peers should be able to look at your finished three ideas and see what "genuinely different" and "properly annotated" actually look like under the same time pressure they're facing.

**Common error to watch for:** restyling one idea three times — same structural concept, different surface details each time.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Is that a different idea or a different colour on the same idea?"* — direct, memorable, and it points a peer straight at the swap test without needing to explain the whole method mid-exam.

</Support>
