The regular class teaches you that a strong function point is specific, testable and user-linked — true, and enough to pass. This session breaks part (a) into the four things a mark scheme actually checks for by name, so instead of testing an answer against three adjectives, you can check it against a fixed list and know exactly which one is missing.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="paper1">Paper 1</ExamLink> is worth 25% of your IGCSE, and part (a) is the first thing you write in the exam — a shaky start costs you time and confidence you don't get back. Today also sets up parts (c) and (e): a precise part (a) makes both of them easier to write.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "User", definition: "The specific person or group who will use the product — named or described precisely enough to picture, not a category. 'A cyclist who commutes daily and stores gear in a small garage' is a user; 'people who cycle' is not." },
  { term: "Design problem", definition: "The specific gap between what currently happens and what the user needs — stated as a problem, not a solution. 'The cyclist's gear is scattered and hard to find before a ride' is a design problem; 'the cyclist needs a shelf' has already jumped to a solution." },
  { term: "Context", definition: "The physical and situational setting the product will exist in — where it's used, when, and under what conditions. A garage, used in low light, in the five minutes before leaving, is a context; 'at home' is not specific enough." },
  { term: "Key product feature", definition: "A feature the given design context specifically calls for or implies — read from the brief, not invented. If the context mentions a switch-operated light, that's a key feature the part (a) answer should identify, not skip." },
]} />

<Section title="Part (a), broken into four checkable things" icon="🔎" id="four-part">

The regular class's "specific, testable, user-linked" test tells you when a function point is weak. This table tells you what a *complete* part (a) answer covers — four separate things, each checkable on its own:

| Component | What to look for in the brief | Common miss |
|---|---|---|
| User | A specific person or group, not a category | Writing "people" or "users" instead of a specific description |
| Design problem | The gap/need, stated as a problem not a solution | Jumping straight to "it needs a shelf" without stating what's actually wrong |
| Context | Where, when, under what conditions | Ignoring situational detail the brief gives you (dim garage, five minutes before leaving) |
| Key features | Features the brief specifically implies (a switch, a specific capacity) | Describing materials or form — that's part (c)/(e) territory, not part (a) |

A part (a) answer that's missing even one of these four is incomplete, however well the other three are written. Reading a past-paper mark scheme with this checklist in hand is the fastest way to see which of the four an examiner is actually crediting in each bullet point.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Auditing a part (a) answer against all four">

**Brief extract:** "A cyclist stores helmet, shoes and tools in a small home garage. Gear is often scattered and hard to find in the five minutes before leaving for a ride. The product should include a switch-operated light for use in low garage lighting."

**Weak answer:** "The product must be strong and hold the cyclist's things. It should look good in the garage."

Audit: User — vague ("the cyclist's things" doesn't establish who). Design problem — missing (never states the scattered/hard-to-find issue). Context — missing (doesn't mention the garage, the low light, or the five-minute window). Key features — missing (never mentions the switch-operated light the brief specifically calls for). Four out of four missing or weak — this answer would score close to zero despite sounding plausible.

**Strong answer:** "The product is for a cyclist who stores gear in a small home garage (user). Gear is currently scattered and hard to locate in the five minutes before a ride (design problem), in a space with poor lighting (context). The product must include a switch-operated LED so gear is visible when loading in low light (key feature), and must organise at least three distinct item types so nothing is missed in a rushed departure."

Every one of the four checklist items is present and traceable to a specific phrase in the brief — this is what "reading the brief precisely" looks like in practice.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: how professionals frame the problem before drawing anything">

Industrial designers spend real project time on exactly this step — sometimes called a "problem statement" or "design brief" — before a single concept sketch exists, because a wrongly-framed problem produces confidently wrong solutions no matter how good the drawing skill is. The discipline is identical to the four-part checklist here: who is this for, what's actually wrong, in what situation, and what does the situation demand — just applied at a professional scale, sometimes over weeks of user research instead of minutes of reading a brief. A precise part (a) is literally practising the first step of real design practice, not just an exam formality.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Four-part audit and rewrite" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["Your returned Term 1 mock script", "A past-paper part (a) mark scheme extract", "Pencil"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Recap the 7-part format and time allocation from the regular class.

**10–25 min — Top-band part (a).** Take a past-paper brief and write a part (a) answer, then audit it yourself against the four-part checklist. Fix any missing component.

**25–35 min — Professional framing, taste.** Read the extension box. Write one sentence connecting the four-part checklist to how a professional would frame the same brief.

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: rewrite a weak part (a) answer to full marks for a past-paper product, then do the same audit-and-rewrite process on your own Term 1 mock's part (a).
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: diagnosing which of the four is missing">

**Common error to watch for:** peers describing materials or form ("it's made of wood, it looks modern") instead of user, problem, context, and features — this is answering part (c)/(e) content in part (a)'s slot.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Which of user / problem / context / key features did you miss?"* — this is more useful than "this needs work," because it points directly at a checkable gap instead of a vague sense that something's wrong.

</Support>
