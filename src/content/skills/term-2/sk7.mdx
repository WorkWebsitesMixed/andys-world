The regular class gives you the kerf-compensation rule as a fact: target slot width = material thickness minus 0.2 mm. This session shows you exactly why that rule exists — what the laser actually removes and why a slot drawn at nominal size ends up loose — and introduces the design principle that rule is one small instance of: designing for manufacture (DFM). It's a **key TA week**: you're a CAD-station helper today, and the fastest fixes are the ones you understand from first principles, not just as a memorised number.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="project">Component 2 Criterion 5</ExamLink> needs evidence of digital tools used to plan for manufacture — and a file that isn't kerf-compensated produces parts that don't fit, which is exactly the kind of avoidable error a CAD-station helper needs to catch before the machine runs, not after.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Kerf", definition: "The width of material the laser beam actually removes as it cuts — typically 0.1-0.3 mm for a school-scale CO2 laser on plywood or acrylic. It isn't a flaw to work around occasionally; it's a fixed physical property of every cut the laser makes." },
  { term: "Design for manufacture (DFM)", definition: "Designing a product's geometry, tolerances, and joints specifically around how it will actually be made — not designing in the abstract and hoping manufacture cooperates.   { term: "Parametric CAD", definition: "CAD where dimensions are defined by variables and relationships (constraints) rather than fixed numbers — change one variable (e.g. material thickness) and every dependent dimension in the model updates automatically, including kerf-compensated slot widths." },
  { term: "CAM toolpath", definition: "The actual path a machine's cutting tool follows to produce a part — for a laser, this is the vector path itself; for a CNC router or 3D printer, the toolpath is a separate calculated path (with more complexity: tool diameter, cut depth per pass, direction) generated from the CAD geometry, not identical to it." },
]} />

<Section title="Kerf compensation: why the rule works" icon="🔬" id="kerf-why">

The regular class's rule (target slot width = material thickness − 0.2 mm) works because of exactly one physical fact: the laser beam has a real width, and as it traces a path, it removes that width of material from *both sides* of the drawn line, not just along the line itself.

<KerfCompensationDiagram />

Draw a slot at exactly 6 mm to fit a 6 mm tab, and the laser removes roughly 0.1 mm from each wall of the slot as it cuts — the slot opens up to roughly 6.2 mm, and the tab now sits loose. Draw the slot at 5.8 mm instead, and the same 0.1 mm-per-wall removal brings the real slot back to 6 mm — a snug fit. This is why the compensation value (0.2 mm total, split across both walls) needs to roughly match your *specific* laser's actual kerf, found by test-cutting a scrap piece — a different machine, power setting, or material can have a meaningfully different kerf, and the 0.2 mm figure is a starting point to verify, not a universal constant.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Verifying kerf on your own machine before committing to real parts">

**The test:** cut a small strip with five slots, each drawn at a different width: 5.6, 5.7, 5.8, 5.9, 6.0 mm, into a scrap piece of your actual material. Test-fit a 6 mm tab into each slot.

**Reading the result:** the slot that gives a snug (not loose, not forced) fit tells you your machine's actual working compensation for this material and thickness — if 5.8 mm is loose but 5.7 mm is snug, your machine's real kerf is slightly wider than the regular class's 0.2 mm default, and every slot dimension in your working file should use 5.7 mm instead.

**Why this matters more than memorising 0.2 mm:** the number itself is machine- and material-specific. The *method* — test, measure, apply — is what transfers to any laser cutter you use in the future, including ones with a completely different kerf.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: DFM, parametric CAD, and CAM toolpaths">

**Design for manufacture (DFM)** is the general principle kerf compensation is one instance of: designing dimensions, tolerances, and geometry around the real behaviour of the process that will make the part, not around an idealised drawing. A DFM-aware designer asks "what will this process actually do to this dimension?" for every critical fit, not just slots — the same question extends to 3D-print tolerances (SK8) and working-drawing tolerances (SK9).

**Parametric CAD** takes this further: instead of drawing a slot at a fixed 5.8 mm, a parametric model defines it as "material thickness − kerf," as variables. Change the material thickness variable from 6 mm to 9 mm, and every slot in the model updates automatically — genuinely useful when a design goes through several material or thickness changes during development, which yours likely has.

**CAM toolpaths** matter more once cutting gets more complex than a laser's simple vector-follows-the-line behaviour — a CNC router, for instance, has to account for the cutting bit's own diameter (the toolpath must offset from the drawn line by the bit's radius) and cut in multiple depth passes for thick material, neither of which a laser needs to worry about.

</Extension>

<Support title="TA prep: the fast CAD-station checks">

**Common error to watch for:** wrong layer or colour assigned to a cut line, or a file drawn in the wrong units (px instead of mm) so every dimension is silently wrong.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Is this drawn to size and ready to cut?"* — a fast, direct question that prompts a peer to actually verify scale and layer assignment rather than assuming the file is correct because it looks right on screen.

</Support>
