<Section title="Section B Structures: the whole engineering chain, under exam conditions" icon="🔗" id="section-b">

T1 SK1 built a four-stage engineering chain: classify → forces → stress/FoS → join. A top-band Section B Structures answer runs that exact chain, connected, under timed exam pressure — not four separate calculations that happen to be on the same page.

<Formula
  name="Direct stress (T1 SK1)"
  equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{F}{A}"
  note="F = force in newtons (N). A = cross-sectional area in mm². Result in N/mm² (= MPa)."
/>

<Formula
  name="Factor of Safety (T1 SK1)"
  equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
  note="Both loads in newtons (N). FoS is dimensionless — a ratio, not a value with units."
/>

<Formula
  name="Moment (T1 SK1)"
  equation="M = F \times d"
  note="F = force in newtons (N). d = perpendicular distance from the force to the pivot/fixing, in metres (m). Result in newton-metres (N·m) — relevant whenever the question describes a cantilever or an off-centre load, not just a simply-supported beam."
/>

**Full working, every time:** write the formula first, then the substitution, then the result with units — the same three-step discipline from T1 SK5, now applied to every one of these in sequence within a single Section B answer. Skipping the formula line loses a method mark even if your final number is exactly right.

</Section>

<Section title="Error-carried-forward: one mistake doesn't sink the whole answer" icon="🔀" id="ecf">

A multi-step Section B question often builds each step on the previous one's result — find the reaction, then use it to find the stress, then use that to find the FoS. If you make an arithmetic slip in step one, **error-carried-forward (ECF)** means every later step is still marked on its own method, using your (wrong) earlier number — not automatically marked wrong because the chain started from an error.

**What this means for exam strategy:** never abandon a multi-part calculation because an early step feels uncertain or you suspect it's wrong. Keep applying the correct method to whatever number you have — the method marks for every later step are still available under ECF, and refusing to continue guarantees losing marks you could have kept.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="A full Section B structures answer, method-marked line by line">

**Question (abbreviated):** A shelf bracket's horizontal arm is fixed to a wall at one end only, 180 mm long, carrying a 45 N load at the free end. The arm's cross-section is 20 mm × 15 mm. The material fails at a stress of 30 N/mm². Calculate (a) the moment at the fixed end, (b) the stress in the arm, (c) the Factor of Safety, and (d) state which structural feature (T1 SK1) the fixing must be designed to resist.

**(a) Moment [2 marks — method + accuracy]**
$M = F \times d$ [method mark: correct formula]
$M = 45\ \text{N} \times 0.18\ \text{m} = 8.1\ \text{N·m}$ [accuracy mark: correct substitution and result with units]

**(b) Stress [2 marks]**
$\text{Stress} = F/A$ [method mark]
$\text{Stress} = 45\ \text{N} / (20 \times 15\ \text{mm}^2) = 45/300 = 0.15\ \text{N/mm}^2$ [accuracy mark]

**(c) FoS [2 marks, ECF-eligible]**
$\text{FoS} = \text{Failure stress} / \text{Working stress}$ [method mark — earned even if part (b)'s stress value was wrong, provided this formula is applied correctly to whatever value part (b) produced]
$\text{FoS} = 30 / 0.15 = 200$ [accuracy mark]

**(d) Structural feature [1 mark]**
The fixing is a **cantilever** fixed end — it must resist both the reaction force AND the 8.1 N·m moment from part (a), unlike a simply-supported beam's two shared supports (T1 SK1). This is exactly the kind of connected reasoning — naming *why* the moment from part (a) matters for the joint, not just stating a joint type — that separates a top-band Section B answer from a collection of four correct but disconnected calculations.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: how engineering exams are actually marked">

The **method marks vs accuracy marks** split isn't arbitrary — it reflects how real engineers are judged too: a correct process applied under pressure that produces a wrong final number due to a slip is a fundamentally different (and less serious) failure than using the wrong method entirely. Mark schemes are built to detect and credit the difference. Professional engineers working under time pressure — in a design review, in a site visit — are trained the same way: show your method clearly enough that a colleague can check it and catch an arithmetic slip before it matters, rather than presenting only a final number that has to be trusted blind.

</Extension>

<Support title="TA prep: diagnosing Section A vs Section B losses">

**After the test**, help peers diagnose exactly where marks were lost: Section A losses are usually a breadth-vocabulary problem (T1 SK5's precise-property-language territory); Section B losses are usually a method problem (missing or incomplete working, not usually wrong content knowledge).

**Your tutoring cue:** *"In Section B, show me your method — even a wrong answer with a right method earns method marks."* This redirects a peer who's discouraged by a wrong final number toward checking whether the method itself (which is where most of the marks actually are) was sound.
