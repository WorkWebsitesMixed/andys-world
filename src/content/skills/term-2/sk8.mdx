The regular class's 3D-print-vs-laser table already tells you when to choose printing. This session gives you the reason orientation matters so much once you've made that choice — the exact mechanism behind SK7's "designing for the process" principle, now applied to a completely different manufacturing process with its own physical constraints. Another **key TA week**: you're helping peers avoid failed prints before they happen, not after.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="project">Component 2 Criterion 5</ExamLink> needs evidence of digital fabrication planning, and a print that fails from bad orientation wastes filament, machine time, and a session — exactly the kind of avoidable failure a bench helper who understands *why* orientation matters can catch before the print starts.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Overhang", definition: "A feature in a 3D-printed part with no material directly beneath it in the current build orientation. Printers build layer by layer, bottom to top — a layer needs the one below it to rest on. Steep overhangs (beyond roughly 45° from vertical) need support material; shallower ones often print fine unsupported." },
  { term: "Support material", definition: "Extra, disposable structure the printer adds beneath an overhang so each layer has something to build on, removed (broken or dissolved away) after printing. Support material costs print time and filament, and leaves a rougher surface where it was attached." },
  { term: "Build orientation", definition: "The direction a part is rotated relative to the print bed before slicing. The same part can need extensive support in one orientation and almost none in another — orientation is a design decision, not a fixed property of the model." },
  { term: "Clearance fit (3D printing)", definition: "The extra gap deliberately added between two mating printed parts so they can move or slide relative to each other, since printed surfaces are never perfectly smooth or dimensionally exact straight off the printer. Typically 0.2-0.3 mm per side for a simple sliding fit on an FDM printer, verified by test — the same 'design around what the process actually does' logic as SK7's kerf compensation." },
]} />

<Section title="Orientation: the same DFM principle, a different process" icon="🖨️" id="orientation">

SK7 established design for manufacture (DFM): design around what the process actually does, not around an idealised drawing. 3D printing's version of that principle is **orientation** — the same part, rotated differently before printing, can be easy or nearly impossible to print well.

<PrintOrientationDiagram />

A horizontal overhang with nothing beneath it forces the printer to build **support material** — a disposable scaffold, printed and then broken away, that costs time, filament, and leaves a rougher surface once removed. Rotating the same part so that feature builds up from the plate directly, rather than hanging in space, can eliminate the need for support entirely. Choosing orientation well is a real design decision, made before slicing, not something the slicer software fixes automatically.

</Section>

<Section title="Tolerances for printing: clearance, the same way kerf worked" icon="📏" id="print-tolerance">

Printed parts, like laser-cut ones, don't come out exactly the size drawn — layer lines, minor dimensional drift, and the nozzle's own width all mean a part modelled at exactly 20.0 mm rarely measures exactly 20.0 mm off the printer. For two printed parts that need to slide or rotate against each other, this means adding a **clearance fit** — a small deliberate gap — the same "design around the real behaviour of the process" logic as SK7's kerf compensation, applied to a different manufacturing method with its own typical figure (roughly 0.2–0.3 mm per side, verified on your own printer, not assumed).

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="A switch bezel: choosing orientation and a tolerance fit">

**The part:** a small bezel that a toggle switch snaps into, sitting proud of the product's front panel with a slight lip.

**Orientation choice:** printed lip-down (flat against the plate), the lip itself is a slight overhang but shallow enough (under 45°) to print cleanly without support. Printed lip-up would leave the lip's underside entirely unsupported, needing scaffolding that would leave a rough surface exactly where the switch needs to snap in cleanly — the wrong choice for a functional fit surface.

**Tolerance fit:** the switch's body measures 12.0 mm across. Modelling the bezel's opening at exactly 12.0 mm would likely need force-fitting or not fit at all once printed. Modelling it at 12.3 mm (0.15 mm clearance per side) is the starting assumption — verified by printing a test bezel first and adjusting if the fit is too loose or still too tight.

**Documented decision:** "Bezel printed lip-down to keep the functional snap-fit surface support-free and clean. Opening modelled at 12.3 mm (0.3 mm total clearance) for the 12.0 mm switch body, verified by test print before committing to the final version."

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: assembly modelling, additive vs subtractive, and FEA revisited">

**Assembly modelling** goes beyond single parts: multiple components are modelled together in one file, with their mating relationships (this face touches that face, this pin fits that hole) defined explicitly — so a change to one part's dimension flags automatically if it breaks the fit with another, rather than being discovered only once both parts are printed. **Additive manufacture** (3D printing — building up material layer by layer) and **subtractive manufacture** (laser cutting, CNC routing — removing material from a larger stock) are fundamentally different processes with different DFM rules: additive struggles with unsupported overhangs but can produce complex internal geometry impossible to cut; subtractive struggles with internal cavities but is fast and cheap for flat or simple 3D shapes. **FEA** (T2 SK4's taste-beyond) applies here too: before committing filament and time to a print, a stress simulation on the CAD model can reveal whether a thin printed feature will actually survive its intended load — the same "test cheaply before committing" principle, one process earlier in the pipeline.

</Extension>

**0–12 min — Preview & demo.** Recap sketch → extrude → modify, 3D-print vs laser, and print setup from the regular class.

**12–28 min — Modelling + print setup.** Model your chosen 3D-print component. Before slicing, rotate it to test at least two orientations and compare support requirements — choose deliberately, not by default.

</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: catching unprintable geometry before it wastes a print">

**Common error to watch for:** a peer's model has a large unsupported overhang in their chosen orientation, or a moving-part fit modelled with zero clearance, and doesn't realise until the print fails or the parts won't move.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"How will this print without collapsing?"* — asked while looking at the model rotated in its planned orientation, before slicing, not after.

</Support>
