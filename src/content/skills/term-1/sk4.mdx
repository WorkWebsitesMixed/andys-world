The regular class hands you a formula — R = (Vsupply − V_LED) / I_LED — and it works. This session shows you *why* it works, by deriving it from Ohm's Law properly, then extends it to something the regular class only offers as an optional homework extension: what happens with two LEDs, in series or in parallel. You'll need both today, because you're the one at the bench when a peer's circuit doesn't light up.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  Every circuit calculation on <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4</ExamLink> is Ohm's Law wearing a different formula — once you see the resistor formula as Ohm's Law applied to one component's share of the voltage, every future circuit question becomes the same three steps, not a new formula to memorise.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Ohm's Law", definition: "The fundamental relationship between voltage, current and resistance in a circuit: V = IR. It applies to any single resistive component, not just 'the resistor' — you can use it on the LED, the resistor, or the whole circuit, as long as you use that component's own voltage and current." },
  { term: "Voltage division", definition: "In a series circuit, the supply voltage is shared between components in proportion to their resistance — each component only 'sees' its own share. This is why the resistor formula subtracts V_LED from Vsupply first: the resistor's own voltage is whatever is left over after the LED has taken its share." },
  { term: "Series circuit (current)", definition: "In series, the SAME current flows through every component — there is only one path. If you add a second LED in series, both LEDs and the resistor all carry the identical current." },
  { term: "Parallel circuit (voltage)", definition: "In parallel, every branch has the SAME voltage across it (both ends connect to the same two points), but the current can differ between branches, and each branch typically needs its own resistor sized for its own component." },
  { term: "Transistor (as a switch)", definition: "A three-terminal semiconductor component that lets a small current into its base terminal control a much larger current flowing from collector to emitter — effectively an electronically-controlled switch, with no moving parts. Used when a small signal (from a sensor, or a microcontroller) needs to switch something that draws more current than the signal source can supply directly." },
]} />

<Section title="Ohm's Law, and why the resistor formula IS Ohm's Law" icon="🔢" id="ohms-law">

<Formula
  name="Ohm's Law"
  equation="V = IR"
  note="V = the voltage across a component, in volts (V). I = the current flowing through it, in amperes (A). R = its resistance, in ohms (Ω). This applies to any single component on its own — the LED has its own V and I, the resistor has its own V and I, and they're not automatically the same numbers."
/>

In the LED circuit, the resistor and the LED are in series, so the same current (I) flows through both — but the supply voltage splits between them: the LED "uses" its own forward voltage (V_LED), and whatever's left over is the resistor's share.

<Formula
  name="The resistor's own voltage"
  equation="V_R = V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}"
  note="V_R = the voltage across the resistor specifically, in volts (V) — not the full supply voltage. V_supply = the battery voltage. V_LED = the LED's forward voltage, fixed by the LED itself regardless of the circuit around it."
/>

Now apply Ohm's Law to the resistor alone, using its own voltage and the shared current:

<Formula
  name="Resistor value (Ohm's Law, rearranged for R)"
  equation="R = \frac{V_R}{I} = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}"
  note="This is the exact formula the regular class gives you — it isn't a separate rule to memorise, it's Ohm's Law (R = V/I) applied specifically to the resistor's own voltage share, with I being the shared series current."
/>

That's the whole derivation. Once you see it this way, any new circuit configuration is just "find each component's own V and I, then apply V = IR" — not a new formula to look up.

</Section>

<Section title="Series vs parallel: what changes when there's a second LED" icon="🔀" id="series-parallel">

The regular class's homework extension asks this question; here's the full method, worked twice.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Two red LEDs: series vs parallel, on a 9 V supply">

Both LEDs: forward voltage 2 V, operating current 20 mA (0.020 A). Supply: 9 V.

**Series (both LEDs share the same current, one resistor):**

<Formula
  name="Series — one resistor for both LEDs"
  equation="R = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED1}} - V_{\text{LED2}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}"
  substitution="R = \frac{9\ \text{V} - 2\ \text{V} - 2\ \text{V}}{0.020\ \text{A}} = \frac{5\ \text{V}}{0.020\ \text{A}}"
  result="R = 250\ \Omega \rightarrow \text{use } 270\ \Omega \text{ (nearest E12 value above)}"
  note="Each LED takes its own 2 V share; the resistor only needs to drop what's left (5 V), because in series the same current passes through everything — one resistor is enough for both LEDs together."
/>

**Parallel (each LED branch has its own resistor, same voltage across both branches):**

<Formula
  name="Parallel — one resistor PER branch"
  equation="R = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{\text{LED}}}{I_{\text{LED}}}"
  substitution="R = \frac{9\ \text{V} - 2\ \text{V}}{0.020\ \text{A}} = \frac{7\ \text{V}}{0.020\ \text{A}}"
  result="R = 350\ \Omega \rightarrow \text{use } 390\ \Omega \text{ (each branch, same as the regular class's single-LED answer)}"
  note="In parallel, each branch sees the full supply voltage independently, so each needs its own resistor sized exactly like a single-LED circuit — you cannot share one resistor between parallel branches, because each branch's current is separate."
/>

**The design consequence:** series uses one resistor but both LEDs go dark together if either fails (they're on the same current path); parallel uses two resistors but each LED is independent — if one fails, the other keeps working. For a product where reliability matters more than component count, parallel is the safer choice, even though it costs one extra resistor.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: transistors, sensors, and where this becomes a system">

A resistor and an LED are a fixed circuit — they do the same thing every time the switch closes. A **transistor** changes that: a small current into its base terminal switches on a much larger current from collector to emitter, so a tiny signal can control something the signal itself couldn't power directly.

<TransistorSwitchDiagram />

The diagram above drives a motor, but the same principle switches a brighter LED array, a buzzer, or anything else that draws more current than a small sensor signal can supply. Combine that with a sensor — an LDR (light-dependent resistor, its resistance falls as light increases) or a thermistor (resistance changes with temperature) — and you have an **input–process–output system** that reacts to the world automatically, rather than only to a switch a person presses. This is a first taste of what a microcontroller does at a much larger scale: sense, decide, act.

</Extension>

<Support title="TA prep: the bench-helper checklist">

**This is a key TA week** — you're the one circulating with a multimeter or just your eyes while peers build on breadboards, and wiring faults are common and fixable if you know where to look.

**Common errors to check for, in order:** (1) LED polarity — the longer leg is the anode (+); (2) missing resistor — a bare LED across the battery burns out in seconds; (3) loose breadboard connections — a wire not fully seated in its row.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Trace the current from + all the way to − — where does it stop?"* — this makes the peer walk the actual current path component by component, which finds a broken connection or a missing part far faster than randomly re-checking everything.

</Support>
