The regular class gives you four annotation types — material, join, function, dimension — and that's genuinely enough to pass. This session adds the ingredient that turns a passing annotation into a distinction one: a **trade-off**. Every real design decision costs something. An annotation that only states the benefit is half a design decision, honestly reported.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="paper1">Paper 1</ExamLink> parts (c) and (e) are annotation-heavy, and examiners can tell the difference between a student reciting a property and a student who understands the decision they made — naming the trade-off is exactly what signals the second one.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Three-part annotation", definition: "Feature + reason + trade-off. Not just what a part is and why it was chosen, but what was given up to choose it. 'MDF — no grain direction, cuts cleanly on the laser cutter — BUT weaker in humid environments than solid timber' is a three-part annotation; stopping after 'cuts cleanly' is only two." },
  { term: "Trade-off", definition: "What a design decision costs, stated honestly alongside its benefit. Every material, joint, or form choice gains something and loses something else — cost, weight, durability, appearance, manufacturing time. Naming the trade-off shows the decision was made with full awareness, not by accident." },

'Horizontal arm, 150 mm — carries the 4 kg load (Spec point 1)' ties the drawing directly back to the C2 specification, which is what examiners and assessors are actually checking the design against." },
]} />

<Section title="From four annotation types to a three-part structure" icon="✏️" id="three-part">

The regular class's four types (material + reason, join + reason, function, dimension) tell you *what* to annotate. The three-part structure tells you *how deep* each one needs to go:

| Annotation depth | Example | What's missing |
|---|---|---|
| One-part (name only) | "MDF" | No reason, no consequence — this is a label, not an annotation |
| Two-part (feature + reason) | "MDF — no grain direction, cuts cleanly on the laser cutter" | True and specific, but reads as if MDF has no downside at all |
| Three-part (feature + reason + trade-off) | "MDF — no grain direction, cuts cleanly on the laser cutter, BUT weaker in humid environments than solid timber, so indoor use only" | Complete: what it is, why it was chosen, and what was knowingly given up |

The regular class's four-type table already gets most students to two-part annotations, which is a real, mark-worthy standard. The trade-off is what an examiner reads as evidence the student actually weighed the decision, rather than reciting a property they memorised.

</Section>

<Section title="Spec-linked annotation: connecting the drawing to C2" icon="🔗" id="spec-linked">

An annotation that only describes the drawing is disconnected from the rest of the folder. An annotation that names the specification point it satisfies proves the drawing and the specification are actually the same design, not two separate documents that happen to sit next to each other:

> "Horizontal arm, 150 mm — carries the 4 kg gear load (Spec point 1), sized to the cyclist's tallest item (Spec point 3) — BUT the longer the arm, the larger the moment the wall fixing must resist (see SK1), so 150 mm is close to the practical maximum before the joint needs upgrading."

Notice this single annotation does four things at once: names the feature, states the reason (with a spec-point reference), states a second reason (also spec-linked), and states the trade-off — with a callback to the structural reasoning from SK1's engineering chain. That's what a distinction-level annotation looks like once form, function, and structure are all connected on the page.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Upgrading a two-part annotation to three-part, spec-linked">

**The sketch:** the same isometric shelf bracket from the regular class's worked example — an L-shaped bracket, 150 mm vertical arm, 200 mm horizontal arm, cut from 18 mm MDF.

**Two-part (regular class standard, mark-worthy):** "Bridle joint reinforced with PVA — mechanical interlock handles bending moment at the shoulder."

**Three-part, spec-linked (distinction standard):** "Bridle joint reinforced with PVA — mechanical interlock handles the bending moment at the shoulder (see SK1's engineering chain: this joint resists a moment, not just shear) — BUT it's permanent once glued, so it doesn't meet a 'must disassemble for recycling' spec point if I add one; if disassembly matters more than joint strength, I'd trade this for a KD fitting instead."

**Why the second version scores higher:** it shows the same technical knowledge as the first, but adds the trade-off (permanence vs strength) AND shows the student weighing it against a specification requirement — exactly the kind of design reasoning C3 and Paper 1 part (c) reward beyond a correct label.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: how professionals annotate under scrutiny">

Patent drawings are annotations under the highest possible scrutiny — every labelled feature must be precise enough that someone else could build the invention from the drawing and the text alone, and every claim about what a feature does must be defensible, because a patent can be legally challenged on exactly that basis. Industrial designers pitching a concept use a lighter version of the same discipline: one annotated page has to carry the whole argument for why a design decision was made, because there's rarely a second document to fall back on. The three-part annotation habit you're building today — feature, reason, trade-off — is the same discipline, at student scale.

<Support title="TA prep: getting past the label">

**Common error to watch for:** an arrow pointing at a feature with just its name written at the end — "timber frame" — with no reason and no trade-off attached at all. This is the single biggest weakness the regular class sees in Paper 1 parts (c) and (e).

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Tell me why you chose that — now write that on the drawing."* Once they've said the reason out loud, a second push — *"And what did you give up to get that? What's the downside?"* — gets them to the trade-off, which is usually the part they can articulate verbally but never think to write down.

</Support>
