The regular class at Week 3 consolidates four Structures sub-topics you already have the foundation for: types and rigidity, forces, loads/moments/FoS, and joining. This session doesn't reteach them — it runs all four as **one connected engineering chain**, applied live to a real project, then hands you the tutoring cue you'll use to help the other 42 do the same thing on Wednesday.

Because SKILLS starts this term at W3, this is also the *first* SKILLS session the eighteen of you attend — and several of you haven't settled on a specific project yet. So before any structures content, this session does something the regular class can't: it forces your brief to exist.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  Structures calculations are the single most heavily-tested area of <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section B</ExamLink>, and the structure you analyse today becomes the load-bearing core of your <ExamLink to="project">Component 2 project</ExamLink>. Get the brief locked and the chain connected now, and both get easier for the rest of the year.
</Callout>

<Section title="Lock the brief" icon="📌" id="brief">

Before any structures content: say your one-line brief out loud, forced into this shape —

> "For **[a named user]**, to **[a specific need]**, carrying **[a real load]**."

No named user and no stated load means it isn't decided yet — "a stand for stuff" is not a brief. If you can't fill in all three blanks specifically, that is today's real priority, because the rest of this session — and Wednesday's regular class — runs the structures analysis on *your own* project, not a generic example.

**Locked, example (this is the standard to hit):**

> "For **my brother Alejandro**, a keen mountain biker who always leaves for school late, to **keep his helmet, shoes and multitool organised and visible in the five minutes before he leaves**, carrying **up to 4 kg of gear on its lowest arm**."

Every blank is specific: a named real person, not "a cyclist"; a need tied to an actual moment in his day, not "storage"; a load with a number attached. This is the same wall-mounted gear organiser used as the worked example later in this session — notice that the load figure it states (4 kg) is exactly the number the structures calculation below runs on. That's the point: a vague brief gives you nothing to calculate.

**Not locked, three near-misses to check yourself against:**

| Brief attempt | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "For cyclists, to store gear." | No named person — "cyclists" is a category, not a user you can picture leaving late for school | Name a specific real person and one specific moment in their day |
| "A helmet rack that holds a helmet." | No load number — "a helmet" isn't a figure you can calculate stress or FoS against | Attach a mass: weigh the actual item(s), or estimate and flag it for refinement |
| "For my dad, to organise his garage, carrying his tools." | Named user, but the load is still vague — "his tools" could be 1 kg or 15 kg | Pick the heaviest realistic single load case and put a number on it, even a rough one |

**Check yourself:** does your load have a number attached (a mass, or at least "the weight of X")? If not, estimate one now — you'll refine it, but you need something to calculate against today.

</Section>

<Section title="The engineering chain" icon="🔗" id="chain">

The regular class teaches these as four sections. They are actually one decision chain, each stage constraining the next:

| Stage | Question you ask | Method | Feeds into |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Classify | Is load carried by members, by a skin, or by solid volume? | Frame / shell / mass | Which force analysis applies |
| 2. Forces | What force does each member carry, and where is it highest? | Tension, compression, shear, bending — identify by inspection | Which member to calculate |
| 3. Stress & FoS | Is the critical member strong enough, with margin? | Stress = Force ÷ Area; FoS = Failure Load ÷ Working Load | Whether the joint needs to resist a force, a moment, or both |
| 4. Join | What joint resists exactly what stage 2–3 found? | Match joint to the dominant force (tension, shear, or moment) | — the point of the whole chain |

The exam trap this table exists to prevent: choosing a joint (say, glue and a butt joint) before you've worked out whether the connection needs to resist a sliding force, a pulling-apart force, or a *rotating* one. Those need different joints, and stage 4 only makes sense once stages 1–3 are done. The rest of this session works through stages 2–4 properly, with the formulas defined term by term, before you run the whole chain on your own project.

</Section>

<Section title="Moments: the concept the regular class doesn't cover" icon="🔄" id="moments">

Every beam calculation you've done so far — including the regular class's simply supported beam — has two supports sharing the load. A **cantilever** only has one: fixed at one end, free and loaded at the other. That single fixed end has to do the job both supports were doing together, which means it must resist two separate things at once: the load itself, and the **moment** the load creates by acting at a distance from the fixing.

A moment is a turning effect: force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from where the force acts to the pivot (here, the fixing point). It's why a long-handled wrench needs less force than a short one to undo the same bolt — same turning effect, bigger distance, smaller force needed. For a cantilever, the load plays the role of the force, and the arm length plays the role of the distance.

<Formula
  name="Moment"
  equation="M = F \times d"
  note="M = the moment, in newton-metres (N·m) — the turning effect at the fixed end. F = the force (the load), in newtons (N). d = the perpendicular distance from the force to the fixing, in metres (m). Doubling either F or d doubles the moment the fixing must resist."
/>

<CantileverDiagram />

This is genuinely new, not just a repeat of the regular class: a simply supported beam's two reactions are just forces (in newtons); a cantilever's single fixed end must resist a force *and* a moment (in newton-metres) — a different unit, describing a different kind of resistance. That distinction is exactly what SelfCheck Question 1 below is testing.

</Section>

<Section title="Stress and Factor of Safety: full method" icon="📐" id="stress-fos">

**Stress** tells you how concentrated a force is inside a material — the same force spread over a small area is far more likely to cause failure than the same force spread over a large one, which is why doubling a member's cross-section doesn't just make it heavier, it directly reduces the stress inside it.

<Formula
  name="Direct stress"
  equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{F}{A}"
  substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{mm}^2}"
  result="\text{Stress} = 10\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 10\ \text{MPa}"
  note="Stress = the internal force per unit area, in newtons per square millimetre (N/mm²), which is the same unit as megapascals (MPa). F = the force acting on the member, in newtons (N). A = the cross-sectional area the force acts over, in square millimetres (mm²). A thicker cross-section spreads the same force over more area, so stress falls even though the force hasn't changed."
/>

**Factor of Safety (FoS)** exists because no material or manufacturing process is perfectly predictable — timber has knots, glue lines vary, a load might briefly spike above what you designed for. FoS is the margin you build in on purpose.

<Formula
  name="Factor of safety"
  equation="\text{FoS} = \frac{\text{Failure Load}}{\text{Working Load}}"
  substitution="\text{FoS} = \frac{200\ \text{N}}{50\ \text{N}}"
  result="\text{FoS} = 4.0"
  note="FoS = Factor of Safety, a dimensionless ratio (no units) — how many times stronger the structure is than it needs to be. Failure Load = the load at which the structure actually breaks or permanently deforms, in newtons (N), usually found by testing. Working Load = the maximum load the structure is designed to carry in normal use, in newtons (N). Higher FoS = a bigger margin, but also more material than strictly needed — structural engineers pick a value (typically 2–6) that balances safety against cost and weight."
/>

Both formulas answer different questions and both feed stage 4: stress tells you whether the *material* is strong enough; FoS tells you how much margin you have. A member can pass on stress and still need a carefully chosen joint — which is exactly what the worked example below shows.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Running the chain on the class example — a cyclist's gear organiser">

The site's running Term 2 worked example is a wall-mounted pegboard frame that must hold a cyclist's helmet, shoes and tools — spec point: **holds 4 kg**. Here's the chain run on it, the way you'll run it on your own project this session.

1. **Classify.** The frame's horizontal support arms projecting from a vertical backboard are the load-bearing members — this is a **frame structure**. The most-loaded arm is a **cantilever**: fixed to the backboard at one end, free and loaded at the other.

2. **Forces.** Worst case: all 4 kg (≈40 N, taking g ≈ 10 N/kg) on the lowest, longest arm, 150 mm from the backboard.

<Formula
  name="Moment at the fixed end"
  equation="M = F \times d"
  substitution="M = 40\ \text{N} \times 0.15\ \text{m}"
  result="M = 6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}"
  note="F = the load at the free end, 40 N. d = the arm length, 0.15 m (150 mm). M = the moment the backboard fixing must resist, 6 N·m. Compare to a simply-supported beam of the same span: there, two supports would share the load between them and there would be no moment to resist at either — here, one joint takes all of it."
/>

3. **Stress & FoS.** The vertical backboard upright carries this load in direct compression. A 20 mm × 20 mm timber upright:

<Formula
  name="Stress in the upright"
  equation="\text{Stress} = \frac{F}{A}"
  substitution="\text{Stress} = \frac{40\ \text{N}}{20 \times 20\ \text{mm}^2}"
  result="\text{Stress} = 0.1\ \text{N/mm}^2"
  note="F = the load carried by the upright, 40 N. A = its cross-sectional area, 20 mm × 20 mm = 400 mm². Timber's compressive strength along the grain is roughly 30 N/mm², so FoS here is about 30 ÷ 0.1 = 300 — the timber is nowhere near failing."
/>

That FoS of 300 is the point of running the whole chain: the raw material is massively over-strong for this load. **The moment from stage 2, not the material stress from stage 3, is the actual design problem** — and only the joint can solve it.

4. **Join.** Screws alone resist the arm sliding out of the backboard (shear) — but the 6 N·m moment wants to *rotate* the arm downward, peeling the screws out rather than shearing them. That needs an **anti-movement detail**: a glued-and-screwed corner block under the arm, spreading the moment over a wide bearing area instead of concentrating it on two screw shanks.

<Section title="Joint selection: matching the joint to the force" icon="🔩" id="joints">

| Joint | What it resists well | Anti-movement detail | Use when the chain shows… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screws / bolts alone | Shear (sliding apart) | None — can work loose under a moment | Pure tension or shear, no significant moment |
| Corner block (glued + screwed) | Shear and moment | Wide bearing face spreads the rotational force | A cantilevered or overhanging member |
| Gusset plate | Shear and moment, at a truss node | Plate ties multiple members together at once | A truss joint carrying several member forces |
| Dowel + glue | Shear, some tension | Glue line resists pull-apart if area is large enough | Panel-to-panel joints, low moment |
| KD (knock-down) fitting | Tension and compression | Mechanical cam/bolt interlock, no glue needed | Where disassembly is a spec requirement |

<StructuralJointsDiagram />

**The rule:** identify the dominant force from stage 2 of the chain first, then pick the joint that resists *that* force specifically — not the joint that's fastest to make or looks the most professional.

</Section>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: the method of sections, and where this goes next">

For a truss with many members, you don't need to solve the whole structure to find one member's force — you use the **method of sections**: imagine cutting straight through the member you care about, then apply equilibrium (sum of forces = 0, sum of moments = 0) to just the piece on one side of the cut. It's algebra, not measurement, and it's how real structural engineers isolate the one member that matters instead of solving everything at once.

The bending you saw qualitatively in the regular class (top of a beam in compression, bottom in tension) can be plotted along the whole length of a member as a **bending moment diagram** — and the shear force at every point as a **shear force diagram**. You won't calculate these at IGCSE, but recognising what they represent is exactly the kind of "beyond" vocabulary that separates a working knowledge of structures from an engineer's.

Real structures use all four stages of the chain at once, continuously: a bridge's deck is a frame in bending, its cables are ties in pure tension, its towers are struts in compression, and every one of those connections was chosen for the specific force it has to resist. A mezzanine floor in a building does the same thing at a smaller scale. That's the chain, at full size.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Lock, tour, apply, TA-prep" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, with HOD circulating" materials={["Your project notes (or a blank page if undecided)", "Calculator", "Squared paper"]}>

**0–8 min — Lock the brief.** Write your one-line brief. No exceptions: named user, specific need, real load with a number. If you're genuinely stuck, the HOD talks to you 1:1 while the rest start the tour.

**8–15 min — Rapid engineering-chain tour.** Walk through the four-stage table as a class, using the cyclist example above as the shared reference.

**15–30 min — Apply the chain to your own structure.** Using your locked brief:
1. Classify your project's main structure (frame / shell / mass) and identify which members are ties and which are struts.
2. Find the reactions (or the moment, if any member is a cantilever) and calculate stress in the heaviest-loaded member.
3. Design and justify a joint for the most critical connection — name the dominant force it must resist and the anti-movement detail that resists it.

If your project is too vague to do this, that's the signal — fix the brief before SK2, not during it.

**30–37 min — Taste beyond.** Method of sections and bending/shear diagrams, as above — a name for where this goes if you carry on past IGCSE.

**37–45 min — TA breadth prep.** Read the TA prep box below and rehearse the cue out loud with a partner before Wednesday.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: covering all four sub-topics">

**Breadth is the challenge this week** — in the regular class you may be asked about any of the four sub-topics, not just the one you find easiest. Prepare a mental map: *"If a peer is stuck on [X], I ask [Y]."*

**Common error to watch for:** treating structures as four isolated vocabulary lists instead of one chain. A peer who can define "frame structure" but can't say what force it carries has memorised stage 1 without connecting it to stage 2.

**Your main tutoring cue:** *"If you know the structure type, what forces must it carry — and where is the stress highest?"* — it forces the peer to move along the chain instead of stopping at a definition.

**A second, quieter cue for this week:** several peers in the regular class will also still be undecided on their project. If one is stuck, ask *"Who is it for, and what's the heaviest thing it has to hold?"* rather than suggesting a product yourself — the brief has to be theirs.

</Support>
