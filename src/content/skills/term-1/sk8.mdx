The regular class gives you five structural principles and SCAMPER to escape design fixation — genuinely effective tools. This session adds two techniques most students never meet before university: **morphological analysis**, which generates ideas systematically instead of by inspiration, and **biomimicry**, which looks outside engineering entirely for structural solutions. Then it gives you the one technique that separates picking an idea from justifying the pick: the **Pugh matrix**.

</Callout>

<Section title="Beyond SCAMPER: generating ideas systematically" icon="🧩" id="morphological">

SCAMPER modifies one existing idea to escape fixation. **Morphological analysis** doesn't start from an idea at all — it starts from the product's sub-functions, and builds ideas by combining options for each:

| Sub-function | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support structure | Cantilever arm | Tension cable | Frame and panel |
| Material | MDF | Mild steel | Plywood |
| Switch | Toggle | Tilt | Push-to-make |

Pick one option from each row and you have a complete concept — "cantilever arm, mild steel, tilt switch" is one combination; "tension cable, plywood, toggle" is a completely different one. A 3-row × 3-option chart like this one already generates 27 possible combinations — far more than a person would generate by sketching from inspiration alone, and several of them will pair options that don't obviously "go together," which is exactly where genuinely novel ideas hide.

</Section>

<Section title="Biomimicry: structural solutions nature already solved" icon="🌿" id="biomimicry">

Nature has been solving structural problems — carrying load with minimum material, resisting impact, joining dissimilar parts — for hundreds of millions of years longer than engineering has existed. **Biomimicry** treats a natural structure as a design reference, not just an aesthetic one.

Three structural examples directly relevant to your project's engineering chain (SK1):

- **Honeycomb** — hexagonal cells give maximum strength-to-weight of any repeating 2D pattern; used in lightweight structural panels (aircraft, flat-pack furniture cores).
- **Bone's internal lattice (trabecular structure)** — bone isn't solid; it's a lattice, denser where load is highest and sparser where it isn't. The Eiffel Tower's ironwork follows the same principle — material placed only where the force analysis says it's needed, not uniformly.
- **Burr seed hooks** — the structure behind Velcro: a mechanical interlock joint, discovered by examining why burrs stuck to a dog's fur under a microscope.

Applying biomimicry to your own project means asking: does a natural structure already solve a version of my load-bearing problem, and what would it look like translated into MDF, timber, or steel?

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Three ideas for the cyclist organiser, one from each new technique">

**Idea from morphological analysis:** combining "tension cable" (support) + "plywood" (material) + "tilt switch" — a plywood shelf hanging from steel cables, LED triggered by a tilt switch when the shelf is loaded (gear placed on it tilts a small sensor plate). A combination unlikely to appear from sketching alone, because "tension" and "tilt-triggered" aren't naturally associated ideas.

**Idea from biomimicry:** a honeycomb-cored MDF panel (laser-cut hexagonal lattice sandwiched between two thin face panels) for the main shelf — maximum strength-to-weight for a wall-mounted structure where minimising the moment on the fixing (SK1) genuinely matters.

**Idea from SCAMPER on idea 1 (regular class technique, for comparison):** substitute the tension cables for a single rigid cantilever arm — same overall concept, different structural principle, produced by modifying rather than generating from scratch.

All three are genuinely different structural principles — exactly what Paper 1 part (c) and C3 both reward, and each came from a different generation method, which is itself worth noting in your annotations.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: choosing between ideas with a Pugh matrix">

Once you have several genuinely different ideas, a **Pugh matrix** selects between them more transparently than a gut feeling — pick one idea as the **datum** (baseline), then score every other idea against each criterion as better (+), the same (0), or worse (−) *relative to the datum only*:

| Criterion | Datum (Idea 1: cantilever) | Idea 2 (tension cable) | Idea 3 (honeycomb panel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holds 4 kg | 0 | 0 | + |
| Fits 400 mm width | 0 | − | 0 |
| Easy to manufacture in school workshop | 0 | − | − |
| Total | 0 | −2 | 0 |

This is a different technique from the T2 evaluation matrix (which scores every idea on an absolute numeric scale) — a Pugh matrix is faster to build under time pressure and makes it immediately visible which idea genuinely outperforms your starting point, which is often a more honest question than "which idea scores highest in isolation." Professional design teams use exactly this method when pitching concepts to a client: the datum is often "what exists already," and every new concept has to earn its place by beating it.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Morphological chart, biomimicry, Pugh matrix, TA-prep" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["A3 paper", "Your specification from SK6", "Pencil"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Recap SCAMPER and the Paper 1 seven-part structure from the regular class as a rapid-fire round.

**10–20 min — Advanced ideation.** Build a 3-row morphological chart for your own project (support structure / material / switch, or your own three sub-functions) and pick two unusual combinations to sketch. Then note one structural principle from nature that might apply to your project's load case.

**20–35 min — Paper 1 project-mapped practice.** Using the morphological and biomimicry ideas above as extra material, draft responses to Paper 1 parts (a)–(c) for your own project — function points, target user, three structurally different annotated ideas.

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: build a Pugh matrix for your own three ideas (pick a datum, score the other two against three criteria from your specification) — keep it, since it's usable evidence for C3's "objective selection" requirement.
</Activity>
