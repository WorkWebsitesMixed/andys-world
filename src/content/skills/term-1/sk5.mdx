The regular class runs M1 today — a graded quiz — and teaches you to strip generic words like "strong" out of your answers. This session goes one layer deeper: it's not just *which words* you use, it's *which command word the question uses*, because "describe," "explain," "justify" and "calculate" each demand a structurally different answer — and answering an "explain" question with a "describe" answer loses marks even when every word in it is technically accurate.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section A</ExamLink> is worth roughly 40 of the paper's marks, and command words are the single most under-taught skill in it — most students revise content but never practise matching their answer's *shape* to the word the question actually uses.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[

<Section title="Command words: four different shapes of answer" icon="🧭" id="command-words">

The regular class's no-generic-words rule fixes *vocabulary*. This table fixes *structure* — what shape your answer needs to be, based on the verb in the question, regardless of how precise your vocabulary already is.

| Command word | What it demands | Minimum structure | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Describe | State features or characteristics, no reasoning required | A list of specific, factual points | Adding "because" reasoning that isn't asked for — wastes time, doesn't lose marks, but isn't needed either |
| Explain | State a fact AND give the reason/mechanism behind it | Fact + "because" + mechanism | Describing without ever saying *why* — a pure description earns none of the explain marks |
| Justify | State a choice AND give reasoning AND (often) a comparison to an alternative | Choice + reason + "compared to X" | Justifying with only one property and no comparison — a "justify" answer needs to show *why this and not that* |
| Calculate | Full numeric working: formula, substitution, result, units | Formula → substitute → answer, with units at every stage | Skipping the formula line — this loses method marks even if the final number is correct |

The exam trap: a student who knows the content cold can still score zero on an "explain" question by writing a perfect "describe" answer — the facts are all correct, but the mark scheme is looking for the causal link the description never makes.

</Section>

<Section title="How mark schemes are actually built" icon="📋" id="mark-schemes">

A real Section A mark scheme lists **indicative content** — the specific points that earn credit — often followed by "or other valid response" or marked **ORA**, meaning the reverse argument also earns the mark. Understanding this structure changes how you revise: instead of trying to memorise one exact phrase, you learn the *shape* of a mark-worthy point, so you can construct an equivalent one under pressure even if it isn't word-for-word what you practised.

**Why "strong" scores 0 but "high tensile strength due to its grain structure" scores 1:** a mark scheme's indicative content specifies a named property (tensile strength, hardness, etc.) — "strong" doesn't specify which property, so it cannot be matched to any single indicative-content bullet. The moment you name the property, you become matchable; adding the mechanism ("due to its grain structure") often unlocks a second mark for explanation depth on top of the identification mark.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="One fact, four command words, four different required answers">

**The fact:** mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa.

**Describe the tensile strength of mild steel.**
✓ "Mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa." — a factual statement is enough; no reasoning required.

**Explain why mild steel is suitable for a structural bracket.**
✓ "Mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa, which means it resists breaking under the tensile forces the bracket experiences when loaded." — the fact PLUS the mechanism linking it to the product's function.
✗ "Mild steel has a tensile strength of approximately 400 MPa." — this is only a description; it never explains *why* that matters for the bracket, so it misses the explain-specific mark.

**Justify the choice of mild steel over aluminium for this bracket.**
✓ "Mild steel's tensile strength (≈400 MPa) is higher than aluminium's (≈270 MPa), so for a heavily loaded bracket, steel gives a larger safety margin against breaking, even though aluminium would be lighter." — choice + reason + explicit comparison + acknowledges the trade-off.
✗ "Mild steel has a tensile strength of 400 MPa, which is high." — no comparison to the alternative, so the "justify" element of the mark is missing even though the fact is correct.

**Calculate the stress in a 20 mm × 20 mm bracket arm under a 40 N load, and state whether it is safe against the 400 MPa tensile strength.**
✓ Full working: formula (Stress = F/A), substitution (40 N / 400 mm²), result (0.1 N/mm²), then the comparison statement ("0.1 N/mm² is far below 400 N/mm², so the arm is safe with a large margin"). Skipping the formula line loses a method mark even if "0.1 N/mm², safe" is the correct final answer.

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: the A-level command-word hierarchy">

A-level exam boards use a more elaborate command-word hierarchy than IGCSE — "evaluate" and "analyse," for instance, demand weighing evidence on both sides and reaching a justified conclusion, one level of reasoning beyond IGCSE's "justify." Recognising that IGCSE's four command words are the foundation of a ladder that keeps going is useful context for anyone continuing D&T, engineering, or any A-level science: the skill of matching answer structure to command word only gets more important, not less.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Command-word drill and mark-scheme construction" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["Five Section A questions across five different topics (provided)", "Mark scheme extracts", "Pencil"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Walk the command-word table together; call out the minimum structure for each of the four words from memory.

**10–25 min — Mark-scheme analysis.** Given three real Section A mark scheme extracts, identify the indicative content points and any ORA/NE notes. For each, write in your own words what specifically would and would not earn the mark.

**25–35 min — Mark-scheme construction, taste.** Take one Section A question and write your own two-line mark scheme for it: one credit-worthy answer and one ORA alternative.

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: take five Section A questions across five different topics, answer each to top band using the correct command-word structure, mark them against the scheme, then write a one-line tip for each that a peer could use.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: finding exactly where marks are lost">

**Your role this week:** sit beside a peer during Section A work in the regular class and identify precisely where their answer earns or loses marks — not just whether the final answer "sounds right."

**Common error to watch for:** a descriptive answer to an "explain" question — all the facts are correct, but there's no "because" connecting them to the question's actual ask.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"What's missing from your answer that the mark scheme would expect?"* — better than telling them the answer, this makes them re-read their own work against the command word.

</Support>
