The regular class at Week 5 gives you the what: concrete is reinforced with steel, composites combine a matrix and a reinforcement, and the 6Rs are six sustainability strategies. This session gives you the why and the where — precisely which part of a beam needs rebar and why it goes exactly there, precisely why fibre orientation matters in a composite, and a genuine number to put behind "sustainable" instead of the word alone.

<Section title="Reinforcement, precisely: where and why" icon="🏛️" id="where">

The regular class tells you concrete is reinforced because it's weak in tension. SK1's engineering chain already gave you the tool to find exactly *where* that tension is: the **neutral axis**. In a loaded, simply supported beam, the top face is in compression, the bottom face is in tension, and the neutral axis runs through the middle at zero stress.

<ReinforcedConcreteDiagram />

That's precisely why rebar sits in the lower zone, close to the bottom face — as far from the neutral axis as possible, on the tension side, where concrete is weakest and steel's tensile strength (≈400 MPa) is needed most. Rebar placed near the neutral axis, or in the compression zone, would do almost nothing — it would be sitting in the part of the beam concrete already handles well.

**Pre-stressed concrete** takes this one step further: steel cables are tensioned *before* the concrete is poured, then released once it has cured. The cables try to shorten, squeezing the concrete into compression everywhere — including the zone that would otherwise be in tension under load. The structure now starts from a compressed state, so it can absorb more tensile load before the concrete ever experiences net tension. This is why pre-stressed beams can span further, or be made thinner, than ordinary reinforced ones.

</Section>

<Section title="Composites: why fibre orientation is the real variable" icon="🧵" id="composites">

A composite's strength isn't just "matrix + reinforcement" — it depends heavily on **which direction** the reinforcement runs, because a fibre only carries load effectively along its own length.

| Fibre layout | Strength along fibres | Strength across fibres | Used when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unidirectional (all fibres one way) | Very high | Low | The load direction is known and fixed (a bicycle frame tube along its length) |
| Woven (fibres in two directions) | High in both weave directions | Lower than unidirectional, but balanced | Load direction varies or isn't fully known (a boat hull, a car panel) |
| Random / chopped strand mat | Moderate, roughly equal in all directions | Moderate | Cheap, general-purpose reinforcement where peak strength isn't critical |

This is why a CFRP bicycle frame's tubes have fibres running along the tube's length (where the main bending and tension loads act), while a boat hull — which takes load from every direction as it moves through water — uses woven cloth. A metal, by contrast, is roughly **isotropic**: equally strong whichever way you test it, because its internal grain structure isn't deliberately aligned the way a composite's fibres are.

</Section>

<Section title="A number behind 'sustainable': embodied energy" icon="🌍" id="lca">

"Recyclable" is not the same question as "low-impact" — a material can be fully recyclable and still expensive to the environment to produce in the first place. **Embodied energy** puts a number on that production cost.

<Formula
  name="Embodied energy of a component"
  equation="EE = m \times ee"
  note="EE = total embodied energy of the component, in megajoules (MJ). m = the component's mass, in kilograms (kg). ee = the material's specific embodied energy, in megajoules per kilogram (MJ/kg) — a published value that differs hugely by material: roughly 8–10 MJ/kg for softwood, 20–35 MJ/kg for mild steel, and 150–200 MJ/kg for virgin aluminium."
/>

That gap between steel and aluminium is the point: aluminium is prized for being lightweight and endlessly recyclable, but producing virgin aluminium from ore is roughly five to seven times more energy-intensive per kilogram than producing steel. A full life-cycle comparison has to weigh that production cost against how much *less* material a lighter design might need — which is exactly the kind of trade-off a "recyclable" label alone doesn't tell you.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="A real LCA comparison: bracket arm, steel vs aluminium">

Using SK2's same bracket arm (20 mm × 20 mm section, 150 mm long) for the cyclist gear organiser — mass roughly 0.14 kg in mild steel (density 7.8 g/cm³), or roughly 0.05 kg in aluminium (density 2.7 g/cm³) at the same size.

<Formula
  name="Embodied energy — mild steel arm"
  equation="EE = m \times ee"
  substitution="EE = 0.14\ \text{kg} \times 25\ \text{MJ/kg}"
  result="EE \approx 3.5\ \text{MJ}"
  note="m = 0.14 kg (the steel arm's mass at this size). ee = 25 MJ/kg, a mid-range published value for mild steel."
/>

<Formula
  name="Embodied energy — aluminium arm"
  equation="EE = m \times ee"
  substitution="EE = 0.05\ \text{kg} \times 175\ \text{MJ/kg}"
  result="EE \approx 8.75\ \text{MJ}"
  note="m = 0.05 kg (the aluminium arm — lighter at the same size, because aluminium is roughly a third the density of steel). ee = 175 MJ/kg, a mid-range published value for virgin aluminium."
/>

**The finding:** even though the aluminium arm uses less than half the mass, its embodied energy is more than double the steel arm's — because aluminium's specific embodied energy is so much higher per kilogram. A justification that says "aluminium is more sustainable because it's lighter" is incomplete without this calculation; a justification that includes it is genuinely distinction-level. (This doesn't mean aluminium is always the wrong choice — where recycled aluminium is used, its embodied energy drops sharply, which is itself a strong sustainability argument if you can source it.)

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: a full life-cycle view">

A true life-cycle assessment doesn't stop at manufacture — it follows the product through raw material extraction, manufacture, transport, use (does it consume energy while in service?), and end-of-life (landfill, recycling, or reuse), quantifying the environmental impact at every stage. Professional LCA software (like SimaPro or GaBi) can compare two entire product designs this way, not just two materials.

<Support title="TA prep: beyond 'recyclable'">

**Common error to watch for:** a peer writes "recyclable" as if that settles the sustainability question on its own. It doesn't — recyclability is about end-of-life, not about the energy cost of making the material in the first place.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"What actually happens to it at end of life?"* — push past the label. If they say "it gets recycled," follow up with *"And how much energy did it take to make in the first place?"* — that's the embodied-energy half of the picture most students never mention.

</Support>
