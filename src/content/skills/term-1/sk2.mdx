The regular class at Week 4 gives you the vocabulary: hardwoods, softwoods, manufactured boards, ferrous and non-ferrous metals, and the working properties that justify a choice. This session doesn't re-teach that list — it takes the one word that ruins most Paper 4 answers ("strong") and forces it apart into the precise, separately-measurable properties an examiner is actually looking for, then goes one property further than the regular class ever gets: **stiffness**, which is not the same thing as strength at all.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="paper4">Paper 4 Section B</ExamLink> routinely asks you to "justify a material choice, referring to two working properties" — and a generic word like "strong" scores zero, no matter how confident the answer sounds. This session gives you the precision that turns a zero-mark answer into a full-mark one, and a genuine engineering distinction (strength vs stiffness) most students never separate.
</Callout>

<Section title="Pulling apart 'strong': four separate properties" icon="🔬" id="precision">

"Strong" collapses at least four independent properties into one vague word — and a material can score high on one and low on another:

| If your justification says… | The precise property is… | Because it's about resisting… |
|---|---|---|
| "It won't snap when pulled/stretched" | Tensile strength | Being pulled apart |
| "It won't crush or buckle under load" | Compressive strength | Being squashed |
| "It won't bend or flex under load" | Stiffness (Young's modulus) | Elastic deformation — NOT the same as breaking |
| "It won't shatter if dropped or knocked" | Toughness | A sudden impact, not a steady load |

Cast iron is the sharpest example of why this split matters: high compressive strength (fine in a column), but low toughness (it shatters, doesn't bend, on impact) — strong by one measure, and the wrong choice wherever impact resistance matters, like a vehicle chassis. "Cast iron is strong" is technically true and completely useless as a justification.

</Section>

<Section title="Strength vs stiffness: the distinction the regular class doesn't make" icon="📏" id="stiffness">

A material's tensile strength tells you the stress at which it *breaks*. Its stiffness tells you how much it *deflects* — bends or stretches — under a load well below that breaking point. These are independent: a material can be strong and flexible (a fishing rod), or strong and rigid (a steel beam), or weak and flexible (a rubber band).

<Formula
  name="Young's modulus (stiffness)"
  equation="E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}"
  note="E = Young's modulus, in gigapascals (GPa) — a measure of stiffness, not strength. σ (sigma) = the stress applied, in N/mm². ε (epsilon) = the strain produced — how much the material stretches as a fraction of its original length (no units; e.g. 0.001 means it stretched by 0.1%). A higher E means the same stress produces less strain — the material is stiffer, deflecting less for the same load."
/>

This is genuinely beyond what the regular class calculates, but the comparison is simple to state: mild steel's Young's modulus is roughly **200 GPa**; aluminium's is roughly **70 GPa** — steel is about three times stiffer, independent of which alloy of either you pick. Two members of identical size and identical load will deflect roughly in inverse proportion to their stiffness, so the aluminium one bends noticeably more even if it's nowhere near breaking.

<WorkedExample title="Same load, same size, same strength margin — different bend">

**The question:** the wall-mounted gear organiser's support arm (150 mm cantilever, 4 kg / 40 N load — the same one from SK1) is made from a 20 mm × 20 mm bar. Compare a mild steel version to an aluminium version of *identical* dimensions.

**Strength check (both pass):** mild steel's tensile strength (≈400 MPa) and aluminium's (≈270 MPa) are both far above the direct stress this load produces (from SK1: ≈0.1 N/mm²) — both have a huge FoS. On a strength justification alone, either material looks fine.

**Stiffness check (this is what actually differs):** steel's Young's modulus (≈200 GPa) is about three times aluminium's (≈70 GPa). Because deflection is inversely proportional to stiffness for the same load and size, **the aluminium arm will sag roughly three times more than the steel one** under the same 40 N load — even though neither is anywhere near breaking.

**Full-mark justification:** "I chose mild steel over aluminium for the support arm because, although both materials have a large safety margin against breaking under this load, steel's Young's modulus (≈200 GPa) is roughly three times aluminium's (≈70 GPa). The arm will visibly sag under 4 kg of gear if made from aluminium at this size, even though it won't fail — steel keeps the deflection small enough that the organiser looks and feels rigid in use."

**Why this scores over a generic answer:** it names two different properties (strength *and* stiffness), states that they answer different questions, and links the numerical difference to a real consequence a user would notice (visible sag) — not just "steel is stronger."

</WorkedExample>

<Section title="Toughness, precisely: the Charpy test" icon="💥" id="toughness">

Toughness can't be measured by pulling slowly — it's about a sudden shock. Real materials testing uses an **impact test**: a raised pendulum is released and swings down to strike a notched sample; how high it swings on the *far* side tells you how much energy the sample absorbed before breaking.

<CharpyTestDiagram />

A tough material (most steels at room temperature) absorbs a lot of energy — the pendulum loses most of its swing. A brittle material (cast iron, most ceramics, some plastics when cold) absorbs very little — the pendulum swings almost as high as it started. This is precisely why "strong" and "tough" are not interchangeable: a material can resist a slow, steady load perfectly well and still shatter the first time something drops on it.

</Section>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: why metals actually deform">

Zoom into a piece of steel and you'd see it isn't one continuous block — it's made of millions of microscopic crystal grains, packed together like an irregular 3D jigsaw. Inside each grain, atoms sit in an ordered lattice, and along certain planes of weakness (**dislocations**), rows of atoms can slip past their neighbours when the material is stressed enough.

That microscopic slipping *is* what "the metal bent" means at the atomic scale — the elastic region on the stress-strain curve above is the lattice stretching without slipping (it springs back); the plastic region is dislocations actually sliding (permanent — it doesn't spring back). Heat-treating a metal (annealing, quenching, tempering) works by changing how easily dislocations can move, which is why the same steel can be made softer and more workable, or harder and more brittle, without changing its chemical composition at all.

<Activity title="Precision properties and the riser challenge" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["Calculator", "Squared paper", "The properties table from the regular class (if already started)"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Walk the "pulling apart 'strong'" table together — practise saying each precise property out loud before writing anything.

**10–25 min — Properties, precisely.** For four material pairs (mild steel / aluminium, oak / MDF, cast iron / mild steel, aluminium / copper), write one sentence each on: which has higher tensile strength, which is stiffer, and which is tougher — using the numbers from this session, not "it depends."

**25–35 min — Stress-strain and dislocations, taste.** Sketch the stress-strain curve from memory, labelling the elastic region, yield point, and UTS. State in one sentence what a dislocation is.

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: **select a material for a riser that must carry 5 kg, and justify it using at least one strength-family property, stiffness, AND a sustainability trade-off** — three separate justifications, not one generic one.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: 'strong in what way?'">

**The single most common error you'll see in the regular class:** a student writes "strong" (or "durable," or "sturdy") with no further qualification. That word alone is worth zero marks no matter how correct the material choice actually is.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"Strong in what way — tension, compression, or against being bent?"* — force the peer to pick one of the four precise properties from the table above and finish the sentence with a "because."

**If they've already named a property but stopped there:** push one step further — *"What would happen to the product if that property were low?"* — that's the "consequence" half every justification needs.

</Support>
