The regular class gives you the three-part specification template — criterion, measure, justification — and today it's graded (M2). This session adds the one word that separates a good specification from a distinction one: **traceable**. Every point in a top-band specification can be followed backwards, in one unbroken chain, to a specific piece of research. If any link in that chain is missing, the point isn't distinction standard yet, however well-written it sounds.

<Callout type="exam" title="Why this matters" id="why">
  <ExamLink to="project">Component 2 Criterion 2</ExamLink> rewards exactly this traceability, and it's assessed today as <ExamLink to="paper4">M2, worth 30% of your term grade</ExamLink> — this session is the difference between a specification that merely follows the template and one that actually earns the top band.
</Callout>

<KeyTerms terms={[
  { term: "Traceability chain", definition: "The unbroken link from a specific research finding, through a specification point, to a named test method. Distinction-level specifications can be read backwards: pick any spec point, and there's exactly one piece of research it came from and exactly one test that proves whether it was met." },
  { term: "Constraint", definition: "A hard boundary condition the product MUST satisfy — not a matter of degree, a pass/fail limit. 'Must not exceed 400 mm in any dimension' is a constraint: violating it isn't a worse design, it's a disqualifying one." },
  { term: "Criterion", definition: "A performance target the product is evaluated against, where doing better is meaningfully better — not a hard pass/fail line. 'Should support as much load as possible above the 5 kg minimum' has a floor (5 kg, itself a constraint) but also headroom above it that counts as genuine improvement." },
  { term: "BS/ISO standard", definition: "A published, independently-verified requirement for a category of product (e.g. furniture stability, electrical safety) — British Standards (BS) or International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Referencing a real standard in a specification is a professional-level justification, because it draws on tested, external evidence rather than the designer's own judgement alone." },
]} />

<Section title="Traceable: the standard the regular class doesn't name" icon="🔗" id="traceable">

The regular class's three-part template (criterion + measure + justification) gets you most of the way to a measurable, justified spec point. **Traceable** adds one more test: can you point to the *exact* research finding that produced this specific number?

| Level | Example | The problem |
|---|---|---|
| Justified, not traceable | "The product must support 5 kg, because the user stores heavy items." | "Heavy items" isn't a specific finding — where did 5 kg come from? |
| Traceable | "The product must support 5 kg, because the user's textbook collection was measured at 4.3 kg on kitchen scales during the Week 1 interview, plus a safety margin." | The number (5 kg) traces to an exact measurement (4.3 kg), from an exact source (Week 1 interview), with the margin explicitly stated as a deliberate addition, not a guess. |

A distinction-level specification survives the question "where exactly did that number come from?" for every single point. If your answer to that question is "I estimated it" or "it seemed about right," the point is justified in form but not yet traceable in substance — go back to the research and either find the number or generate it (measure something, ask the user a follow-up question).

</Section>

<Section title="Constraints vs criteria: not every requirement is the same kind" icon="⚖️" id="constraints-criteria">

Real engineering requirements documents separate these two explicitly, and mixing them up weakens a specification's precision:

| | Constraint | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Hard boundary — pass/fail | Performance target — degree matters |
| Example | "Must fit within 400 × 400 mm" | "Should hold as much load as practically achievable above the 5 kg minimum" |
| Consequence of missing it | The design is disqualified, however good it is otherwise | The design scores lower, but is still valid |
| How to write it | State the exact limit, no room for interpretation | State the floor (which is itself a constraint) AND the direction of "better" |

Most weak specifications write everything as if it were a criterion ("should be as sustainable as possible") when some of it is actually a hard constraint the design cannot violate at all ("must operate below 50 V DC — SELV safety limit"). Separating the two in your own head, even if the write-up doesn't use these exact words, sharpens which points are truly non-negotiable.

</Section>

<WorkedExample title="Making one spec point fully traceable, with a real standard">

**Weak:** "The product must be safe."

**Justified but not fully traceable:** "The product must have rounded edges, tested by running a finger along all edges, because sharp edges are dangerous."

**Traceable, with a real standard:** "The electrical circuit must operate at 12 V DC, verified by checking the power supply rating, because any voltage below 50 V DC meets the SELV (Safety Extra-Low Voltage) standard referenced in BS EN 61140 — this is a **constraint**, not a target, since exceeding it disqualifies the design regardless of any other benefit." The traceability chain here is complete: standard (BS EN 61140) → specific limit (50 V DC) → the design's actual value (12 V DC, well within it) → test method (checking the power supply rating).

</WorkedExample>

<Extension title="Taste beyond: what a professional requirements document looks like">

Engineering requirements documents in industry go further still: every requirement gets a unique ID, a rationale field (the traceability chain, formalised), a verification method (test, inspection, analysis, or demonstration), and is explicitly tagged as either a constraint or a target. Some organisations even trace requirements forward to which test in the verification plan proves each one — the same "trace it backward, trace it forward" discipline you're practising today, just with more formal paperwork around it.

</Extension>

<Activity title="Traceability audit and the rewrite challenge" time="45 min" grouping="Individual, HOD circulating" materials={["Your research notes and specification draft from the regular class", "Pencil"]}>

**0–10 min — Preview.** Recap primary vs secondary research, ACCESS FM, and the three-part template from the regular class.

**10–25 min — Distinction-level spec.** For three of your own specification points (or the teacher-provided weak examples if yours aren't written yet), trace each one backward: name the exact research finding it came from. If you can't, that point isn't traceable yet — fix it now.

**25–35 min — Standards taste.** Read the constraints-vs-criteria table. For your own project, identify one requirement that is a hard constraint (cannot be violated) and one that is a criterion (has headroom for improvement above a floor).

**35–45 min — TA cue rehearsal + Challenge.** Read the TA prep box, then complete the challenge: rewrite three weak specification points into fully measurable, justified, AND traceable ones, each with a named test.
</Activity>

<Support title="TA prep: 'how would you measure whether it passed?'">

**Common error to watch for:** a peer writes "must be strong" (or similarly vague) as a specification point — this fails at the very first hurdle, before traceability is even relevant.

**Your tutoring cue:** *"How would you measure whether it passed?"* — if they can't answer with an actual test (a number, a tool, a procedure), the point isn't a specification yet, it's a wish.

**Once they have a measure:** push one step further with *"Where did that number come from?"* — that's the traceability check, and it's what separates a good spec from a distinction one.

</Support>
