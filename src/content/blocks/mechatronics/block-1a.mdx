A team of five spends five weeks building a real Da Vinci-inspired paddle boat — a monohull with twin side paddle wheels on a wooden gear train — for the EIA Challenge, then races it on a lake. The team splits into two tracks that share every hands-on build day: a **design trio** who take the boat from paper sketch to a cut-and-assembled wooden hull, and an **electronics pair** who build the firmware up in stages, from pairing a Bluetooth gamepad to a fully autonomous run with a hardware safety layer.

## The two tracks

| | Who | Attends | Main technical focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Design trio** | 3 students | Tuesdays | Hull, paddle wheels, wooden gear train, waterproofing, notebook |
| **Electronics pair** | 2 students | Tuesdays + Thursdays | Firmware: pairing, motor drive, servo, RC integration, modes/failsafe, autonomy |

Both groups get exposure to the whole boat — the electronics pair works the physical hull alongside the trio at every joint session, and the trio gets a hands-on systems-overview walkthrough of the electronics early on, so every student can explain the whole boat to a judge, even though deep firmware work stays with the pair.

## The engineering stack

The boat runs on an **ESP32-WROOM-32E** with an onboard **SS6625E** motor driver, controlled by an **Xbox controller over Bluetooth** via the **Bluepad32** library — not Wi-Fi or a router. A second ESP32 and **ESP-NOW** (peer-to-peer, connectionless radio) come in only as a reserve, if the lake day shows Bluetooth's ~10–15 m range doesn't cover the real course. A hardware arm switch and a mode switch (RC ↔ autonomous) sit between the controller and the motors as a safety layer that can't be bypassed by software.

## What you build

A single-motor RC paddle boat with a wooden twin-paddle-wheel drive and a laser-cut, waterproofed hull. A Bluetooth gamepad on shore controls throttle and steering in real time; a physical switch flips it into a fifteen-second autonomous run. Race day is the EIA Challenge lake event.
